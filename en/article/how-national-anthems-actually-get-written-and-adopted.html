How National Anthems Actually Get Written and Adopted

    • Every national anthem started as an ordinary piece of writing or music before a government decided it deserved to represent an entire country. Some began as marching songs for revolutionary armies, others as court compositions written on royal commission, and a surprising number were adopted almost by accident after decades of informal use before any law made them official.

    Looking at how anthems actually come into being reveals a process that is far less ceremonial and far more improvised than the solemn occasions on which anthems are eventually played. Understanding that process also explains some of the odder facts about anthems: why some have no words, why a handful of unrelated countries once shared the exact same tune, and why the anthem you hear today may be a heavily revised version of something written for a completely different political era.

    Most Anthems Were Not Written to Be Anthems

    A large share of the world's national anthems began life as something else — a patriotic poem published in a newspaper, a hymn written for a church service, a march composed for a specific military regiment, or a song performed at a single public event that happened to catch on. The composer or poet, in most of these cases, had no idea their work would eventually be sung before heads of state and sporting crowds.

    This informal origin explains why the authorship of many anthems is contested or only loosely documented. Melodies passed through years of oral performance and amateur arrangement before any government took formal ownership of them, so the version eventually adopted often differs meaningfully from whatever the original composer actually wrote.

    Only in the twentieth century, particularly around waves of decolonization, did commissioning an anthem specifically as a national symbol become a common and deliberate act of state-building, with governments treating the anthem as a designed piece of national branding rather than an organically adopted folk tradition.

    Who Actually Writes the Words and Music

    There is no single professional path to writing a national anthem. Military bandmasters, employed to compose marches and ceremonial music for the armed forces, wrote a substantial number of the anthems still in use, since they were often the only trained composers available to a young state at the moment an anthem was needed.

    Poets and playwrights supplied the words for many others, sometimes decades before any music was attached, with a separate composer later setting an already well-known poem to a melody chosen specifically to fit its existing rhythm and meaning.

    In a smaller number of cases, a single individual is credited with both words and music, and in a handful of unusual instances the anthem's origin is genuinely uncertain, with historians only able to trace it back to an approximate decade and region rather than a named author.

    How a Song Formally Becomes an Anthem

    Formal adoption typically happens through one of three routes: a specific act of parliament naming the piece as the official national anthem, a royal or presidential decree in systems where the head of state holds that authority, or gradual codification of a piece that had already been used informally at state functions for years before any law caught up with the practice.

    The legal instrument matters because it determines how easily the anthem can later be changed. An anthem fixed by constitutional provision generally requires a more demanding process to alter than one set by ordinary statute or ministerial regulation, which is part of why some anthems have persisted essentially unchanged for well over a century while others have been revised several times within a single generation.

    Some countries never fully complete this legal step, continuing to treat a piece as the de facto anthem through consistent state use — playing it at the Olympics, printing its lyrics in school curricula, performing it at military ceremonies — without ever passing a specific law formally naming it, which occasionally produces genuine ambiguity about what the true legal anthem actually is.

    Anthems Written by Public Competition

    A number of countries, particularly newly independent states seeking a clean symbolic break from a colonial or previous political era, have selected their anthem through an open public competition rather than a single commissioned composer, inviting citizens to submit both lyrics and melody for judged selection.

    These competitions serve a dual purpose beyond simply finding suitable music: they generate public engagement with the new national identity and allow the government to claim the resulting anthem genuinely reflects popular sentiment rather than being imposed from above by a small governing elite.

    The practical results have been mixed. Some competition-selected anthems have become beloved and enduring national symbols, while others were revised again within a decade once the initial patriotic enthusiasm of independence gave way to more pragmatic assessment of the winning entry's actual musical and lyrical quality.

    Countries With No Official Lyrics at All

    A small number of national anthems exist purely as instrumental compositions, with no officially sanctioned set of words at all, even though unofficial or popularly sung lyrics sometimes circulate informally alongside the melody at sporting events and public gatherings.

    This choice is rarely accidental. In countries where multiple official languages or ethnic communities coexist, agreeing on a single set of lyrics that does not implicitly privilege one language or group over another can be politically difficult enough that leaving the anthem wordless becomes the least contentious available option.

    In other cases, the melody itself predates any serious attempt to write matching lyrics, and by the time the anthem's status had become firmly established through decades of instrumental-only use, there was little practical appetite to retrofit words onto a tune the public already strongly associated with silence rather than singing.

    Anthems That Started as Something Else Entirely

    Several current national anthems were originally written for an entirely different purpose than eventual national representation — a few began as opera arias, others as popular folk songs with no political content whatsoever, and at least one well-known anthem started life as a birthday song for a monarch rather than a piece intended to represent an entire nation.

    Repurposing existing, already-popular music offered a practical advantage to newly formed governments: the tune was already familiar to the population, dramatically reducing the effort required to spread recognition compared with introducing an entirely unfamiliar piece of music from scratch.

    This repurposing occasionally created awkward tension when the original, non-national meaning of a melody remained culturally present alongside its new anthem status, requiring governments to actively manage public understanding of the piece's now-primary patriotic role.

    Why Anthems Get Rewritten or Replaced

    Anthems are most commonly rewritten or replaced at moments of genuine political rupture — independence from colonial rule, the fall of a monarchy or dictatorship, reunification after division, or a formal transition between fundamentally different systems of government.

    Lyrics that once celebrated a specific ruling family, a now-discredited ideology, or a version of national identity that explicitly excluded part of the current population are the most frequent targets for revision, since retaining them risks turning a supposedly unifying national symbol into an ongoing source of division.

    Musical revision, as distinct from lyrical revision, is considerably rarer, since melodies tend to accumulate genuine popular attachment over time even when the political content of accompanying lyrics becomes contested, which is why many revisions retain the original tune while replacing only the words.

    Anthems Born Out of Independence Movements

    Many anthems in use today were composed specifically during an active independence or liberation struggle, often performed clandestinely or in exile before the movement achieved formal statehood, which gives these particular anthems an unusually direct and traceable origin compared with older, more organically evolved examples.

    These liberation-era anthems frequently retain lyrics explicitly referencing the struggle itself — resistance, sacrifice, and the specific historical circumstances of independence — giving them a more overtly political character than anthems adopted through calmer, more incremental processes decades or centuries after a country's founding.

    Because the composers of liberation-era anthems were often themselves political or military figures rather than professional musicians, several of these anthems carry simpler musical structures than older European-style anthems, prioritizing memorable, easily sung melodies suited to being learned quickly by a mobilized population.

    Anthem Traditions Across the Gulf and Arab World

    National anthems across the Gulf and wider Arab world reflect a mix of origins broadly similar to the global pattern — some composed by state-affiliated musicians shortly after independence or unification, others with older roots as ceremonial or tribal music later formalized into national status as modern statehood developed through the twentieth century.

    Several anthems in the region emphasize themes of loyalty to the ruling leadership, national unity, and religious or cultural heritage, reflecting both the historical role of leadership in state formation and the broader cultural weight placed on continuity and stability within the region's political traditions.

    As with anthems elsewhere, several regional anthems have been revised since their original adoption, whether to update references following political changes such as federation or unification, or simply to standardize an official arrangement and set of lyrics after years of variation in how the piece was performed at different official occasions.

    Copyright, Trademark, and Who Owns an Anthem

    Most national anthems in active use today are old enough that their original composition has entered the public domain under standard copyright terms, meaning the underlying melody and lyrics can generally be freely performed, arranged, and reproduced without seeking permission or paying royalties.

    Newer anthems, or anthems that have received an officially commissioned modern arrangement, can carry more active copyright protection on that specific arrangement even while the underlying older melody remains in the public domain, creating a layered ownership situation that occasionally surprises people assuming anthems are automatically free of any restriction.

    Separately from copyright, some governments assert trademark-style control over the anthem's use in explicitly commercial contexts — advertising, branded merchandise, or for-profit performance — while still permitting unrestricted use in education, civic ceremonies, and non-commercial public life.

    The Protocol Around Playing an Anthem

    Most countries maintain formal or strongly conventional protocol governing when and how the anthem should be played, including its use at the opening of official state functions, military ceremonies, school assemblies, and the arrival or departure of visiting heads of state.

    Expected audience behaviour — standing, facing a flag if one is present, removing headwear, maintaining silence — is frequently set out in law or official guidance, though the degree to which this protocol is actively enforced against ordinary citizens varies enormously between countries, from strict legal penalty to purely informal social expectation.

    Broadcasters and state media typically follow separate, more specific protocol regarding which authorized arrangement or recording of the anthem may be used in official broadcasts, partly to maintain a consistent, dignified official presentation and partly to prevent unauthorized reworkings that a government might consider disrespectful.

    Anthems at the Olympics and World Cup

    International sporting bodies including the International Olympic Committee require each participating nation to submit an approved, standardized recording and printed score of its anthem well in advance of competition, specifically to avoid disputes or last-minute confusion over which version should be played during a medal ceremony.

    These sporting contexts have occasionally forced genuinely difficult diplomatic decisions, including cases where political disputes over recognition of a territory's status required organizers to substitute a neutral piece of music in place of a disputed national anthem, or to withhold a flag while still permitting an athlete to compete under a separate designation.

    The emotional intensity surrounding anthem performance at major sporting events has, in practice, done more to cement public attachment to a specific arrangement and tempo of a national anthem than almost any other single recurring occasion, occasionally influencing which version a government subsequently designates as its preferred official recording.

    Melodies Borrowed or Shared Between Countries

    A small number of unrelated countries have, at different points in history, used the exact same melody for their respective national anthems, typically because a widely admired composition by a famous European composer was independently adopted by more than one government before international coordination made such overlap embarrassing enough to actively avoid.

    In most documented cases of a shared melody, one of the countries involved has since adopted a distinct replacement anthem specifically to eliminate the overlap, though the historical fact of the earlier shared tune remains a frequently cited piece of anthem trivia.

    Regional political unions and federations have occasionally considered, and in rare cases briefly adopted, a shared anthem intended to represent the union as a whole alongside each member state's individual anthem, though such shared symbols have generally proven less durable than individual national anthems rooted in a single country's specific history.

    Unofficial and Regional Second Anthems

    Beyond the single legally designated national anthem, many countries maintain one or more widely recognized unofficial anthems — patriotic songs with no formal legal status that nonetheless carry deep cultural weight and are performed regularly at public and sporting events alongside, or occasionally instead of, the official anthem.

    Federal or devolved political systems frequently produce regional anthems representing a specific state, province, or autonomous region, performed at local ceremonies and events without any claim to replacing the single national anthem used at the federal level.

    These secondary and regional anthems generally coexist peacefully alongside the official national anthem, though tension can arise when a regional anthem carries separatist associations that a national government views as implicitly challenging national unity rather than simply celebrating local identity.

    Translating an Anthem Without Losing Its Meaning

    Multilingual countries face a genuine practical challenge in deciding whether an anthem should exist in a single official language or in officially sanctioned parallel versions across multiple languages, a decision that carries real symbolic weight about which linguistic communities the anthem is understood to represent.

    Direct translation of anthem lyrics frequently proves difficult because the original wording was chosen partly for its rhythm and rhyme to fit the existing melody, meaning a literal translation often scans poorly when sung, forcing translators to balance fidelity to the original meaning against genuine musical singability in the new language.

    National anthems endure not because any single origin story makes them inevitable, but because sustained, repeated public performance over generations gradually converts even an accidental or improvised piece of music into something a population comes to experience as inseparable from its own sense of collective identity. Whether an anthem began as a soldier's march, a poet's private verse, or the winning entry in a public competition, its final authority comes from decades of ceremony rather than from the circumstances of its composition.

    That is ultimately why so many anthems have such improvised, occasionally accidental origin stories: the formal act of adoption almost always came after a melody or set of words had already begun doing the cultural work of representing a nation, with the law simply catching up to and confirming a status the public had, in practice, already granted.

    FAQ

    Who actually writes a national anthem?

    Anthems have been written by poets, army bandmasters, court composers, and in several modern cases by public competition; there is no single professional route, and many anthems were not originally written with national status in mind.

    Can a country have a national anthem with no official lyrics?

    Yes — a handful of countries have adopted a purely instrumental anthem, either because multiple languages made a single official lyric politically difficult or because the melody predates any agreed words.

    Do national anthems have copyright protection?

    Most anthems are old enough to be in the public domain, but newer or officially arranged anthems can carry copyright or trademark-style restrictions on commercial use, even though the government generally permits free use for civic and educational purposes.

    Why do some countries change their national anthem?

    Anthems are usually replaced or rewritten after independence, a change of political system, or when existing lyrics come to be seen as tied to a discredited regime or excluded part of the population.

    Is there an official etiquette for national anthems?

    Most countries set formal protocol in law or convention covering when the anthem is played, how long a version may run at state events, and expected audience behaviour such as standing, though enforcement varies widely.
