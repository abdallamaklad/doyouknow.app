How Carbon Offset Programs Actually Work

    • When an airline offers to "offset" the emissions from your flight for a few extra dollars, or a company announces it has reached "net zero," a carbon offset is almost always doing the underlying work. The idea sounds simple: pay someone else to reduce or remove a ton of carbon dioxide somewhere in the world, and count that reduction against your own emissions. In practice, the market that has grown up around this idea is a lot more complicated, and considerably more contested among researchers, than the marketing language usually suggests. Understanding how an offset is actually created, verified, and sold explains both why the concept is appealing and why so many scientists and journalists have raised serious doubts about whether it delivers what it promises.

    What a Carbon Offset Actually Represents

    A carbon offset is a tradable credit that represents one metric ton of carbon dioxide, or an equivalent amount of another greenhouse gas, that has supposedly been kept out of the atmosphere or removed from it by a specific project somewhere in the world.

    Buying an offset does not reduce the buyer's own emissions; it is a claim that someone else's emissions reduction, achieved elsewhere, can be counted against the buyer's carbon footprint instead, on the theory that a ton of carbon dioxide has the same climate effect no matter where it's emitted or avoided.

    This substitution logic is the entire foundation of the offset market, and it only works if the underlying reduction is real, additional, and permanent — three conditions that researchers studying the market have found are frequently difficult to verify in practice.

    The Basic Mechanics of an Offset Transaction

    A typical offset transaction starts with a project developer — often a company specializing in forestry, renewable energy, or methane capture — that designs an activity intended to reduce or remove greenhouse gas emissions relative to what would have happened otherwise.

    The developer then applies a methodology approved by a carbon credit registry to estimate how many tons of emissions the project will avoid or remove over a defined period, and after review, the registry issues that number of credits, which can then be sold on the open market.

    Buyers, ranging from individual travelers offsetting a flight to multinational corporations offsetting their entire operations, purchase and "retire" these credits, meaning the credit is taken permanently out of circulation so it cannot be resold or counted twice.

    How Offset Projects Get Approved

    Before a project can issue credits, it typically must be registered with one of several independent carbon credit standards, such as Verra's Verified Carbon Standard or the Gold Standard, which set the methodologies projects must follow and the evidence they must submit.

    Registration generally requires the developer to submit a detailed project design document explaining the proposed activity, the baseline scenario it's being measured against, and the calculations used to estimate emissions reductions, all of which is meant to be reviewed before approval.

    Once approved and operating, projects are supposed to undergo periodic monitoring and verification to confirm they are performing as claimed, though the frequency and rigor of this ongoing oversight varies considerably between registries and project types.

    The Role of Verification Standards and Registries

    Independent third-party auditors, who are accredited by the registries themselves rather than by governments, are responsible for verifying that a project's claimed emissions reductions match reality before credits are issued or renewed.

    This auditor-pays-registry, registry-pays-auditor structure has drawn criticism from some researchers, who note it creates a potential conflict of interest: auditors are paid by the project developers whose claims they are supposed to be independently checking.

    Several major registries have responded to this criticism by tightening accreditation requirements and introducing additional spot-checks, though the underlying structural tension between commercial incentives and rigorous verification remains a subject of ongoing debate in the field.

    Avoidance Credits vs Removal Credits

    Offset credits generally fall into two broad categories: avoidance credits, which claim to prevent emissions that would otherwise have occurred, and removal credits, which claim to actively pull existing carbon dioxide out of the atmosphere and store it.

    Avoidance credits, such as those from protecting a forest that might otherwise have been logged, are generally considered harder to verify with confidence, since they depend on a counterfactual — what would have happened without the project — that can never be directly observed.

    Removal credits, such as those from direct air capture facilities or verified reforestation with measurable biomass growth, are generally viewed by climate scientists as more scientifically defensible, though they currently represent a small fraction of the overall offset market compared to avoidance credits.

    Forestry and Land-Use Offset Projects

    Forestry and land-use projects, including efforts to prevent deforestation, known as REDD+ projects, make up one of the largest categories of the voluntary offset market, generating credits based on estimates of carbon stored in trees that would otherwise have been cut down.

    These projects have faced some of the sharpest scientific scrutiny in the market, since estimating how much deforestation a project actually prevented requires modeling a hypothetical future that didn't happen, a task that multiple peer-reviewed studies have found registries have sometimes significantly overestimated.

    A widely cited 2023 investigation by journalists working with researchers at several universities examined a large sample of rainforest protection credits and found that a substantial majority showed little to no evidence of the deforestation reductions the credits claimed, a finding the registry involved disputed but that prompted broader reform discussions.

    Renewable Energy and Methane Capture Projects

    Renewable energy projects, such as wind and solar installations in regions still reliant on fossil fuel power, generated a large share of early offset credits, based on the argument that the clean electricity displaced power that would otherwise have come from burning coal or gas.

    As renewable energy has become cost-competitive with fossil fuels in many markets without any offset subsidy, several major registries have phased out or restricted new renewable energy credits, on the grounds that these projects would often have been built anyway, undermining the additionality argument.

    Methane capture projects, which collect gas from landfills, livestock operations, or coal mines that would otherwise vent directly into the atmosphere, are generally regarded as easier to measure accurately than forestry projects, since methane volume can often be metered directly at the capture point.

    The Compliance Market vs the Voluntary Market

    Compliance carbon markets, such as the European Union Emissions Trading System, are created and regulated by governments, requiring specific industries to hold enough credits to cover their emissions or face financial penalties, with generally tighter rules than voluntary markets.

    The voluntary market, where companies and individuals buy offsets to support sustainability claims rather than to meet a legal requirement, has grown rapidly but operates with far less standardized oversight, since no single regulator sets binding rules across every registry and project type.

    Some researchers who study carbon markets argue that voluntary offsets have absorbed most of the criticism directed at "carbon markets" broadly, even though compliance markets, which are more tightly regulated, generally show more consistent and verifiable emissions outcomes.

    The Core Problem: Additionality

    Additionality is widely considered the single hardest problem in offset verification: a credit is only meaningful if the emissions reduction would not have happened anyway without the offset revenue, a condition that is inherently difficult to prove because it requires comparing reality to a hypothetical alternative.

    Project developers have a direct financial incentive to claim strong additionality, since credits from projects that clearly would have happened regardless carry no real climate benefit, creating a structural tension between the people estimating additionality and the people benefiting financially from a favorable estimate.

    Several academic studies analyzing large samples of approved offset projects have found that a meaningful share likely failed to meet a strict additionality standard, though estimates of exactly how large that share is vary significantly depending on methodology and project type studied.

    The Core Problem: Permanence

    Permanence refers to whether a carbon reduction or removal actually lasts, which is a particular concern for forestry projects, since a protected forest that burns in a wildfire, is illegally logged, or is cleared decades later effectively releases the carbon the credit claimed to have locked away.

    Registries generally address this risk through "buffer pools," where a percentage of credits from forestry projects are set aside and not sold, intended to cover losses if some protected areas are later damaged or destroyed, functioning similarly to an insurance reserve.

    Whether these buffer pools are large enough to cover real-world losses, especially as wildfires become more frequent and severe in a warming climate, remains genuinely contested among researchers, with some studies suggesting current buffer levels may be inadequate for the scale of risk involved.

    The Core Problem: Leakage and Double Counting

    Leakage occurs when protecting one area from deforestation simply shifts the logging or land-clearing activity to a nearby unprotected area, meaning the total emissions reduction is smaller than the project claims, or in some cases close to zero once the shifted activity is accounted for.

    Double counting happens when the same emissions reduction is claimed by more than one party — for example, both the host country's national emissions inventory and the company that purchased the offset credit — inflating the apparent total climate benefit beyond what actually occurred.

    International climate negotiators addressed double counting directly in the Paris Agreement's Article 6 framework, which sets rules for how countries must adjust their own emissions accounting when credits generated within their borders are sold internationally, though implementation is still being worked out.

    Why Independent Investigations Found Weak Offsets

    Beyond the widely cited rainforest credit investigation, multiple independent research groups and investigative journalists have published analyses over the past several years questioning the real-world impact of specific large offset projects, particularly in the forestry and land-use category.

    These investigations generally don't argue that offsetting as a concept is inherently fraudulent, but rather that quality varies enormously between projects, and that credits from weaker projects have historically traded at similar prices to credits from stronger, more rigorously verified ones.

    This price convergence between high- and low-quality credits is itself viewed by market researchers as a signal of a functioning market failure: buyers often cannot easily distinguish credit quality at the point of purchase, reducing the financial incentive for developers to invest in more rigorous, costlier verification.

    How Prices Are Set and Why They Vary So Much

    Offset prices vary enormously, from under two dollars per ton for some older, lower-quality forestry credits to well over one hundred dollars per ton for high-permanence removal credits like direct air capture, reflecting both differences in verification quality and the underlying cost of the reduction activity itself.

    Compliance market prices are generally set by supply and demand within a capped trading system regulated by government policy, while voluntary market prices are set more informally, through direct negotiation or broker platforms, with far less price transparency for outside observers.

    Market analysts tracking voluntary carbon credit prices have noted growing price stratification since the rainforest credit investigations became widely publicized, with buyers increasingly willing to pay a premium for credits from projects with stronger verification and more transparent monitoring data.

    Corporate Net-Zero Claims and Offset Dependence

    Many corporate "net zero" or "carbon neutral" claims rely heavily on purchasing offsets to cover emissions the company hasn't yet reduced directly, a practice that climate policy researchers have increasingly scrutinized as sometimes substituting for, rather than supplementing, actual emissions cuts.

    Advertising and consumer protection regulators in several countries have taken enforcement action against companies for offset-based sustainability claims deemed misleading, generally on the grounds that the underlying credits didn't represent the verified, additional reduction the marketing implied.

    Climate scientists broadly agree that offsets, even high-quality ones, cannot substitute for direct emissions reductions at the scale required to meet international climate targets, and are better understood as a tool for addressing genuinely hard-to-eliminate emissions rather than a substitute for cutting easier ones.

    Efforts to Reform Offset Quality Standards

    In response to sustained criticism, several major registries and industry bodies have launched reform efforts, including the Integrity Council for the Voluntary Carbon Market, which has developed stricter quality benchmarks that projects and credit types must meet to earn a quality label.

    These reforms generally focus on tightening additionality testing, improving permanence safeguards for forestry credits, and increasing transparency around project-level monitoring data, so buyers can more easily distinguish higher-quality credits from weaker ones before purchase.

    Whether these voluntary reform efforts are sufficient without binding government regulation remains a genuinely open question among policy researchers, some of whom argue that meaningful market-wide quality improvement will require external oversight rather than self-regulation by the registries themselves.

    What Researchers Say Offsets Can and Can't Do

    Climate researchers generally agree that offsets can play a legitimate role for emissions that are currently very difficult to eliminate directly, such as certain industrial processes or aviation fuel, provided the underlying credits meet a genuinely rigorous additionality and permanence standard.

    What most researchers reject is the idea that offsets can serve as a general-purpose substitute for reducing emissions at the source, since the science is clear that avoiding a ton of emissions in the first place is more reliably effective than trying to counterbalance it after the fact with an offset of uncertain quality.

    The practical takeaway most experts converge on is that offset quality varies enormously, that buyers should scrutinize verification standards and project type rather than trusting price or marketing language alone, and that offsetting works best as a complement to, not a replacement for, direct emissions cuts.

    FAQ

    What does buying a carbon offset actually do?

    It funds a project elsewhere that claims to reduce or remove a ton of carbon dioxide, and lets the buyer count that reduction against their own emissions, but it does not reduce the buyer's own output.

    Are all carbon offsets equally reliable?

    No. Quality varies enormously between project types and registries, and independent investigations have found some forestry credits showed little evidence of the claimed emissions reductions.

    What is 'additionality' in carbon offsets?

    It's the requirement that an emissions reduction would not have happened without the offset revenue, a condition that's inherently hard to prove and a major source of criticism of the market.

    Can companies rely on offsets instead of cutting emissions?

    Climate researchers generally say offsets shouldn't substitute for direct emissions cuts, and regulators in several countries have taken action against misleading offset-based sustainability claims.

    Are removal credits better than avoidance credits?

    Removal credits, like direct air capture, are generally viewed as more scientifically defensible than avoidance credits, though they currently make up a small share of the overall offset market.
