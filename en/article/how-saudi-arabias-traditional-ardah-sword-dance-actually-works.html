How Saudi Arabia's Traditional Ardah Sword Dance Works

    • What Ardah Means and Where the Word Comes From

    Ardah comes from the Arabic root meaning "to display" or "to present." It describes a men's performance combining rhythmic swaying, drumming, and recited poetry, historically staged before tribal fighters set out or returned from battle in the Najd region of central Arabia.

    The word itself signals the dance's original purpose: a public display of a tribe's strength, unity, and readiness. That display function survived even after the fighting stopped being real, which is why the performance still looks and sounds like a rehearsal for war.

    Two Facing Lines Form the Basic Structure

    Performers organize into two parallel rows facing each other, sometimes numbering dozens of men per side. The rows sway forward and back in unison, closing the gap between them and retreating again, mirroring the advance and pullback of two opposing forces.

    This formation is not decorative choreography. It reproduces the physical layout of a tribal muster, where men lined up shoulder to shoulder before marching. The symmetry communicates discipline and collective identity rather than individual skill.

    Drums Set the Rhythm the Whole Group Follows

    A set of large frame drums, generically called tabl, drives the tempo that every other element locks onto. Drummers typically stand at the center or move along the lines, and the beat determines how fast the rows sway and step.

    Without the drum line, the dance loses its cohesion almost immediately, since dozens of men are coordinating movement purely by ear. The drummers are treated as core performers, not background musicians, and skilled drummers are recognized within their communities.

    The Sword Is Ceremonial, Not a Combat Tool

    Each performer carries a straight sword, held upright or swung in controlled arcs timed to the drumbeat. The blades are real but the movements are choreographed for safety, with no contact between performers and no combat sequences.

    The sword's role today is symbolic: it references the dance's martial origin without recreating actual fighting. Some regional variants substitute canes or sticks for swords, especially in performances involving very young participants learning the steps.

    A Reciter Delivers Poetry Between the Drumbeats

    A designated poet or reciter, sometimes called al-mehawreb, chants verses that pause or interweave with the drumming. These verses often praise the tribe, the ruler, or the occasion being marked, and can be composed for the specific event.

    The reciter's role requires memorization and often improvisational skill, since verses are adapted to whoever is being honored. This oral component is why UNESCO's official listing names dance, drumming, and poetry together as one inseparable practice.

    Nabati Poetry Gives Ardah Its Verbal Content

    The verses recited during Ardah typically follow the Nabati tradition, a colloquial form of Arabic poetry historically composed and transmitted orally by Bedouin and tribal communities across the Arabian Peninsula, distinct from classical, formally metered Arabic verse.

    Nabati poetry covers themes of honor, lineage, and hospitality, which map directly onto what Ardah performances celebrate. Because it is oral and colloquial, it has remained accessible to ordinary participants rather than requiring formal literary training.

    The Dance's Original Function Was Military Muster

    Before Saudi Arabia's modern unification, Najdi tribes performed Ardah to rally fighters ahead of raids or battles, and again on return to mark victory or mourn losses. The performance served a coordinating and morale function, not just entertainment.

    Historical accounts describe Ardah accompanying tribal leaders as they announced military campaigns, with the recited poetry naming allies, rivals, and battle objectives. This gave the dance a practical, almost administrative role inside tribal warfare.

    How the Ritual Shifted From War to Celebration

    As tribal warfare declined with the consolidation of the Saudi state in the twentieth century, Ardah's occasion shifted from actual military mobilization to symbolic celebration. The same movements now open state ceremonies, weddings, and national holidays instead of battles.

    This transition preserved the dance's form while emptying its original stakes. The swords, drums, and lines remained essentially unchanged; what changed was the meaning attached to them, from a real call to arms to a performed memory of one.

    Najdi Ardah Became the Nationally Adopted Style

    Because the House of Saud's political base originated in Najd, the Najdi style of Ardah became the version most associated with the Saudi state itself and the one performed at national-level ceremonies, rather than a purely regional folk tradition.

    This national adoption is reflected in UNESCO's official title for the practice, "Alardah Alnajdiyah," which specifically names the Najdi variant rather than Ardah as a general Arabian genre, distinguishing it from related dances found elsewhere in the Gulf.

    Other Saudi Regions Have Their Own Dance Traditions

    Najdi Ardah is not the only traditional dance in Saudi Arabia. The Hijaz region has its own performance styles, and the southern provinces of Asir and Jazan practice distinct dances such as al-khatwa and al-mizmar with different steps and instruments.

    These regional forms often use different instruments, such as the mizmar wind instrument in some southern and Hijazi dances, alongside or instead of frame drums, and their movements are generally more athletic and individualized than the collective sway of Najdi Ardah.

    How Najdi Ardah Compares to Southern Dance Forms

    Where Najdi Ardah emphasizes slow, synchronized swaying by static lines, southern dances like al-khatwa often involve faster stepping patterns, individual displays of agility, and different vocal styles, reflecting distinct historical and geographic influences within the Kingdom.

    The contrast illustrates that Saudi folk dance is not a single monolithic tradition but a set of regional practices, each tied to local tribal history, terrain, and social occasions, with Ardah standing out mainly because of its adoption at the national level.

    The Dance Is Passed Down Across Generations

    Ardah is typically taught informally, with boys learning the steps and drum rhythms by observing and joining older male relatives at family and community gatherings, rather than through formal lessons or written notation of any kind.

    This generational chain is treated as essential to keeping the tradition authentic. Cultural heritage organizations in Saudi Arabia have highlighted the risk of losing informal transmission as urbanization changes how extended families gather and spend time together.

    Costume Elements Complete the Performance

    Performers typically wear the thobe, the standard ankle-length garment, often paired with a bisht, a formal open cloak, and a ghutra or shemagh head covering. Some also carry a curved dagger, or khanjar, tucked into a belt as a traditional accessory.

    The clothing signals formality and occasion rather than a special dance costume separate from everyday dress. This matters because it reinforces that Ardah is treated as a ceremonial extension of normal social life, not a theatrical performance in costume.

    Weddings and Graduations Now Host the Dance

    Ardah is commonly performed at family celebrations such as weddings and graduation ceremonies, where it signals joy and communal pride rather than any military connotation. Guests of honor are sometimes invited into the lines as a gesture of respect.

    This everyday use keeps the tradition alive outside of large state events, embedding it in ordinary Saudi social life. It also means most Saudis encounter Ardah first through family occasions rather than through television broadcasts of national ceremonies.

    National Day on September 23 Features Ardah Prominently

    Saudi National Day, marking the 1932 unification of the Kingdom under King Abdulaziz, is one of the largest annual occasions for public Ardah performances, staged in city squares and broadcast nationally alongside fireworks and other celebrations.

    These performances are often large-scale, involving dozens or hundreds of participants organized by local municipalities or cultural authorities, turning what was once a tribal-scale ritual into a mass civic spectacle watched across the country.

    Founding Day, Established in 2022, Also Showcases Ardah

    Saudi Arabia introduced Founding Day as an official holiday in 2022, observed on February 22, to commemorate the founding of the first Saudi state in 1727 rather than the 1932 unification marked by National Day.

    Ardah performances feature heavily in Founding Day events as well, since the holiday deliberately emphasizes older Najdi tribal heritage predating the modern Kingdom, making the dance's tribal-era origins directly relevant to the occasion's historical framing.

    Saudi Leaders Have Personally Joined the Dance

    Saudi kings and senior officials have periodically joined Ardah lines during state ceremonies, a gesture that signals participation in shared tribal heritage rather than mere spectatorship, reinforcing the dance's role as a unifying national symbol.

    Foreign heads of state have also been invited to join, most visibly during a 2017 state visit by a sitting U.S. president to Riyadh, when a sword dance ceremony was staged as part of the official welcome.

    Ardah Functions as a Diplomatic Welcome Ritual

    Beyond domestic celebrations, Ardah is sometimes staged specifically to welcome visiting foreign dignitaries, positioning the dance as a form of cultural diplomacy that presents Saudi heritage to an international audience during high-profile state visits.

    These diplomatic performances tend to be more elaborate and larger in scale than everyday family versions, often involving professional or semi-professional troupes rather than spontaneous participation, precisely because they are meant to represent the country formally.

    The Janadriyah Festival Popularized Ardah Nationwide

    The National Festival for Heritage and Culture, commonly known as Janadriyah and held annually near Riyadh, has served for decades as a major public showcase for Ardah alongside other regional folk arts, crafts, and tribal displays.

    The festival draws large domestic and international audiences and gives troupes from different provinces a shared stage, which has helped standardize public awareness of Ardah as a recognizable national symbol rather than a purely local Najdi custom.

    UNESCO Inscribed Ardah as Intangible Heritage in 2015

    UNESCO added "Alardah Alnajdiyah, dance, drumming and poetry in Saudi Arabia" to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2015, formally recognizing the combined practice rather than any single one of its components alone.

    The listing describes Ardah as a practice performed at social occasions to express happiness and solidarity, transmitted informally within families and communities, which matches how the tradition actually functions in Saudi daily and ceremonial life today.

    Ardah Was the First Saudi Element on UNESCO's List

    The 2015 inscription made Ardah the first cultural practice from Saudi Arabia to receive UNESCO intangible heritage recognition, opening the way for later Saudi inscriptions such as Al-Qatt Al-Asiri wall art and traditional Sadu weaving.

    Being first mattered symbolically for Saudi cultural policy, since it established an international benchmark for how the Kingdom documents and nominates its heritage practices, a process that has continued as Saudi Arabia has expanded its cultural diplomacy efforts.

    Cultural Institutions Now Document and Teach Ardah

    Bodies such as heritage preservation societies and, more recently, government cultural agencies created under Vision 2030 have taken on roles in documenting Ardah's regional variations, training performers, and organizing public performances beyond informal family settings.

    This institutional involvement is relatively recent compared to the dance's centuries-old informal transmission, reflecting a broader Saudi policy shift toward formally cataloguing folk traditions before they risk being diluted by urbanization or generational disengagement.

    Ardah Remains a Men-Only Performance Tradition

    Ardah, in its Najdi form, is performed exclusively by men, consistent with its historical origins as a martial and tribal ritual in which women did not take part directly in the public display of the dance.

    This exclusivity has remained largely intact even as the dance moved from war ritual to civic celebration, meaning women's participation in Saudi folk performance traditions has developed through separate, parallel dance forms rather than joint Ardah lines.

    Women Have Separate Folk Performance Traditions

    Saudi women maintain their own distinct folk dance and musical traditions, performed at gender-segregated gatherings such as wedding celebrations, which are documented and celebrated separately from Ardah rather than folded into it.

    These traditions vary by region just as men's dances do, and cultural documentation efforts in Saudi Arabia have increasingly worked to record them alongside male-performed dances like Ardah, rather than treating men's heritage as the default national tradition.

    Similar Sword and Line Dances Exist Across the Gulf

    Ardah-style dances are not unique to Saudi Arabia. Related performances involving lines of men, drums, and sometimes swords or sticks exist in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the UAE, reflecting a shared Bedouin and tribal heritage across the Gulf.

    Each country has developed its own name and stylistic details for these dances, and some Gulf states have pursued their own heritage recognition for local variants, which sits alongside rather than in competition with Saudi Arabia's Najdi-specific UNESCO listing.

    What Sets the Saudi Najdi Version Apart

    The Saudi Najdi Ardah is distinguished by its specific pairing of two static, swaying lines with a central or roaming poet-reciter, and by its formal adoption as a symbol of the Saudi state's own founding narrative and royal ceremonies.

    This state-level adoption is the clearest distinguishing feature: while related dances elsewhere remain primarily community or tribal practices, Najdi Ardah carries an added layer of national symbolism tied directly to the modern Kingdom's founding and its ruling family.

    Ardah Regularly Appears at Major Public Events

    Beyond national holidays, Ardah performances have opened large public events in Saudi Arabia, including sporting and entertainment occasions, as organizers use the dance as a recognizable cultural marker when presenting the country to broad domestic and global audiences.

    This visibility has increased alongside Saudi Arabia's growing calendar of major international events since the mid-2010s, giving Ardah more frequent exposure on television and online than it received when it was confined mainly to regional festivals and family gatherings.

    Vision 2030 Ties Ardah to Broader Heritage Policy

    Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030 economic and social reform program includes an explicit emphasis on cultural heritage as a pillar of national identity and tourism development, and folk practices like Ardah fit directly into that broader policy framework.

    Under this framework, heritage festivals, cultural agencies, and tourism campaigns have incorporated Ardah more deliberately as a visible symbol of Saudi identity aimed partly at international visitors, alongside its continued role in purely domestic celebrations.

    Modern Safety Standards Shape How Ardah Is Staged

    Large-scale public Ardah performances today follow organized safety practices, including trained handling of real swords, controlled crowd spacing, and event coordination by municipal or festival organizers, differing from the informal, spontaneous family versions of the dance.

    These adaptations matter because they allow the tradition to scale up for stadium-sized or televised events without changing its core movements, meaning the dance's essential structure of lines, drums, sword, and poetry has stayed stable even as its staging context grew larger.

    Professional Troupes Have Emerged Alongside Family Performance

    As demand grew for staged performances at festivals, diplomatic events, and televised ceremonies, semi-professional and professional Ardah troupes formed in several Saudi cities, rehearsing formations and drum patterns rather than relying solely on spontaneous family gatherings.

    These troupes coexist with the older informal tradition rather than replacing it; most Saudi men who perform Ardah at weddings or local events still learn informally, while troupes are called on specifically for large-scale or high-visibility occasions.

    Historians Use Ardah's Poetry as a Historical Record

    Because Ardah verses historically named real tribal leaders, alliances, and conflicts, researchers studying pre-unification Najdi history sometimes treat recorded or remembered Ardah poetry as a supplementary oral source alongside written chronicles and travelers' accounts.

    This use is limited by the fact that verses were often improvised and rarely written down in real time, so historians generally treat surviving Ardah poetry as illustrative of tribal values and events rather than as a precise chronological record.

    Ardah Now Functions as a National Identity Symbol

    Across state ceremonies, diplomatic welcomes, family celebrations, and tourism campaigns, Ardah has settled into a role as a widely recognized shorthand for Saudi cultural identity, one of the few folk practices performed at nearly every level of Saudi public life.

    Its continued relevance rests on the same structure that has defined it for generations: two facing lines, a driving drumbeat, ceremonial swords, and recited poetry, adapted from an actual call to battle into a call to shared national and communal pride.

    FAQ

    Is the sword used in Ardah a real weapon?

    Yes, performers typically carry real straight swords, but the choreography is designed so there is no contact between performers, and no combat is simulated.

    When was Ardah recognized by UNESCO?

    UNESCO inscribed "Alardah Alnajdiyah, dance, drumming and poetry in Saudi Arabia" on its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2015, the first Saudi element to receive that recognition.

    Can women perform Ardah?

    Najdi Ardah is traditionally a men-only performance. Saudi women maintain their own distinct folk dance and music traditions performed separately at gender-segregated gatherings.

    Where does the word Ardah come from?

    Ardah derives from an Arabic root meaning "to display" or "to present," reflecting the dance's original purpose of publicly showing a tribe's strength and unity.

    What instruments are used in Ardah?

    Large frame drums, generically called tabl, set the main rhythm. The performance also relies on recited poetry rather than melodic instruments as its second core element.

    Is Ardah only performed on National Day?

    No. Ardah is performed year-round at weddings, graduations, Founding Day, National Day, diplomatic welcomes, and various cultural festivals such as Janadriyah.

    Did any foreign leaders ever join an Ardah performance?

    Yes. Foreign dignitaries have joined Ardah lines during state visits, most notably during a 2017 visit by a sitting U.S. president to Riyadh, where a sword dance was staged as a welcome ceremony.

    What is Nabati poetry?

    Nabati poetry is a colloquial, orally transmitted form of Arabic poetry historically composed by Bedouin and tribal communities across Arabia, distinct from formally metered classical Arabic verse. It is the poetic style typically recited during Ardah.

    Is Najdi Ardah the only sword dance in Saudi Arabia?

    No. Other Saudi regions, including Hijaz, Asir, and Jazan, have their own distinct folk dances, such as al-khatwa and al-mizmar, which differ from Najdi Ardah in steps and instruments.

    How is Ardah traditionally taught?

    It is passed down informally, with boys learning the steps and rhythms by observing and joining older male relatives at family and community gatherings, without formal lessons or written notation.

    What does UNESCO's inscription actually protect or require?

    UNESCO's Representative List recognizes a practice as significant intangible heritage and encourages safeguarding measures like documentation and transmission support, but it does not impose legal restrictions on how the practice is performed.

    Are similar sword dances found in other Gulf countries?

    Yes. Related line dances involving drums and sometimes swords or sticks exist in Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the UAE, reflecting shared Bedouin and tribal heritage across the Gulf region.

    Why is Ardah associated specifically with the Saudi royal family?

    Because the House of Saud's political base originated in Najd, the Najdi style of Ardah became closely tied to the ruling family's own heritage and is performed regularly at royal and state ceremonies.

    What is the difference between Founding Day and National Day performances?

    National Day, September 23, marks the 1932 unification of the Kingdom. Founding Day, February 22, commemorates the first Saudi state established in 1727. Both feature Ardah, but Founding Day emphasizes older tribal-era heritage.

    Has Ardah changed much from its original war-time form?

    The core structure of lines, drums, sword, and poetry has stayed largely unchanged. What changed is the context and meaning, shifting from real military mobilization to symbolic celebration at civic and family occasions.
