Why Game Soundtracks Are Composed Before the Game Is Finished

    • When a film composer sits down to write a score, they usually work against a locked, or nearly locked, cut of the movie, matching music precisely to scenes that already exist in their final form. Video game composers rarely get that luxury. Most of a game's soundtrack is written years before the game ships, often while levels are still rough gray-box layouts, characters are placeholder capsules, and entire mechanics are still being redesigned. Understanding why game music gets made this way, so far ahead of a finished product that doesn't yet exist in any stable form, reveals just how different game composition is from writing for film or television, and why the tools built to handle this uncertainty have become some of the most sophisticated in modern music production.

    There Is No Finished Cut to Score Against

    A film has an edited timeline: shots exist in a fixed sequence, scenes have a defined length, and a composer can literally watch the movie and place music against a timecode. A video game has no equivalent fixed timeline, because how long a player spends in any given area, room, or encounter depends entirely on that individual player's choices, skill, and pacing.

    This fundamental difference means a composer cannot simply write a three-minute cue timed to a three-minute scene, because the "scene" in a game might last ninety seconds for one player and fifteen minutes for another, depending on how they explore, fight, or get stuck. Music has to be built to survive and adapt to that uncertainty rather than assume a fixed duration.

    Because of this, composers are often asked to think less in terms of individual finished cues and more in terms of flexible musical systems, modular pieces that can loop, layer, transition, and recombine depending on what's actually happening in the game at any given moment, which requires planning that begins long before any specific scene is locked.

    Working from Concept Art and Design Documents

    In the earliest stages of a game's development, long before there's playable content to look at, composers frequently work from concept art, mood boards, and written design documents describing a world, character, or emotional tone that doesn't exist yet in any tangible form. A composer might receive a paragraph describing a decaying underwater city and a handful of atmospheric reference images, and from that alone begin sketching musical themes.

    This early involvement is deliberate: game directors often want music to help define the identity of a world or character while that identity is still being figured out, rather than treating music purely as a finishing touch applied after everything else is locked. A strong early theme can actually influence how artists and writers continue developing a character or setting, creating a genuine back-and-forth rather than a one-way handoff.

    Working this far ahead of finished visuals requires composers to develop a kind of imaginative trust in the design team's vision, translating abstract descriptions and rough sketches into concrete musical ideas that will later need to survive contact with an actual, playable game that may look and feel quite different from the original concept art.

    Placeholder Builds and Gray-Box Levels

    Much of the actual composing happens against what developers call gray-box or white-box builds: unfinished levels made of simple geometric blocks standing in for buildings, terrain, and props, often with no final art, lighting, or character models in place. A composer might be asked to write triumphant boss-battle music for an arena that is, visually, still just a gray rectangular room.

    This requires composers to rely heavily on written briefs, verbal direction from the game director, and their own imagination to bridge the gap between the placeholder visuals in front of them and the polished, atmospheric final environment the music needs to eventually support. It's a genuinely unusual creative constraint compared to scoring finished footage.

    As development progresses and placeholder art gets replaced with final assets, composers often revisit and refine earlier cues to make sure they still match the mood of the now-finished environment, since a piece written for an abstract gray box doesn't always land the same way once real lighting, texture, and atmosphere are in place.

    Why Music Has to Be Modular, Not Linear

    Unlike a film score, which plays in one fixed sequence, most modern game music is composed as a set of interlocking modules: short musical stems, layers, and transition pieces designed to be recombined dynamically depending on what's happening during play, rather than one continuous piece meant to be heard start to finish in order.

    A single combat encounter, for example, might have a low-intensity exploration layer, a mid-intensity layer that fades in as enemies notice the player, and a high-intensity layer for the climax of the fight, all composed in the same key and tempo so they can crossfade smoothly into one another as the situation escalates or de-escalates in real time.

    Designing this kind of modular system requires the composer to think architecturally from the very start of the process, planning how pieces will connect to each other before any of them are fully written, which is a fundamentally different creative process from writing a single, fixed piece of music meant to be heard exactly the same way every time.

    Adaptive Audio Middleware Makes It Possible

    The technical backbone that makes this modular approach practical is specialized audio middleware software, tools designed specifically to let composers and audio designers define rules for how musical layers should trigger, blend, and transition based on real-time gameplay data, without requiring a programmer to hand-code every musical change.

    These tools let a composer specify, for instance, that a particular musical layer should fade in when a player's health drops below a certain threshold, or that a transition sting should play the instant a boss enters its second combat phase, all triggered automatically by the game engine reporting that state change to the audio system in real time.

    This middleware effectively turns the composer into a systems designer as much as a musician, someone who has to think not just about what notes to write but about the underlying logic and conditions that will determine when and how those notes actually get heard by any individual player during their unique playthrough.

    The Constant Back-and-Forth of Revision

    Because games are built iteratively, with mechanics, level layouts, and pacing frequently changing throughout development, music written early often has to be revised, sometimes extensively, as the game itself evolves around it. A cue composed for a level that later gets redesigned, shortened, or cut entirely may need to be reworked or repurposed elsewhere.

    This iterative relationship goes both directions: just as level design changes can force music revisions, a particularly strong piece of early music can influence how a level or encounter gets redesigned, with directors sometimes extending or restructuring a section of gameplay specifically to give a favorite musical moment more room to land.

    This constant revision cycle is one of the clearest ways game composition differs from film scoring, where music is generally written once against a picture that, by the time the composer is hired, is largely finished and unlikely to change significantly before release.

    Composing for Player Choice and Branching Paths

    Many modern games allow players to approach content in different orders, make narrative choices that branch the story, or explore open worlds with no fixed sequence at all, and music has to be composed with all of that unpredictability in mind from the outset rather than assuming players will experience content in any particular order.

    This means a composer often can't write music tied tightly to one specific narrative moment the way a film composer would, since that moment might occur at wildly different points for different players, or might not occur at all depending on choices made earlier in the game. Themes instead tend to be built around characters, locations, or emotional states that can recur flexibly across many different contexts.

    Open-world games take this further still, often requiring large libraries of ambient and exploratory music designed to loop comfortably for extended, unpredictable stretches of time without becoming repetitive or fatiguing, since a player might spend two minutes or two hours in the same general area depending entirely on their own pace and interests.

    How Recording Sessions Get Scheduled So Early

    Because orchestral recording sessions, particularly ones involving large ensembles and professional recording facilities, require significant advance booking and coordination, composers often need to have substantial portions of a score finished and ready to record well before the game itself is anywhere close to completion.

    This scheduling reality pushes composition timelines even earlier than creative considerations alone would demand, since a composer working toward a booked recording date has a hard external deadline that doesn't move just because the game's development has slipped or a level got redesigned again.

    As a result, some of a game's most memorable orchestral themes may have been fully composed, recorded, and mixed a year or more before the level or cutscene they eventually accompany was even close to its final form, representing a significant creative bet made well ahead of seeing the finished product they're meant to serve.

    The Risk of Writing for Something That Might Change

    Composing this far ahead of a finished product carries real creative risk: a theme written for a character concept that later gets significantly rewritten, or for a game mechanic that ultimately gets cut during development, may need to be discarded entirely or substantially reworked to fit whatever the game eventually becomes.

    Experienced game composers generally learn to write with a degree of flexibility built in from the start, favoring themes and motifs that can be reorchestrated, retempoed, or recontextualized relatively easily, rather than composing something so tightly tied to one specific early vision of the game that it becomes unusable if that vision shifts.

    This need for built-in flexibility is part of why many game composers develop musical themes as short, adaptable motifs rather than long, fully fixed compositions early in a project, saving the more elaborate, fully orchestrated arrangements for later stages once the game's direction has stabilized enough to justify that level of investment.

    Why This Approach Produces Distinctive Game Music

    This unusual production process, composing early, working from incomplete information, and building for interactive flexibility rather than fixed sequence, is part of why game music often sounds and functions differently from film or television scoring, even when performed by the same orchestras and produced to the same technical standard.

    Game composers have to write music that remains emotionally effective across dozens or hundreds of possible playthroughs rather than one fixed viewing experience, which pushes many toward strong, memorable, flexible themes that can carry emotional weight even when heard in a different order, at a different length, or blended with other musical layers than originally planned.

    The constraint of uncertainty, in other words, has genuinely shaped the art form itself, encouraging a style of composition built around modularity, thematic flexibility, and responsiveness to player action that has no direct equivalent in more traditional, linear visual media.

    What Players Actually Experience as a Result

    For the player, this entire complex production process is usually invisible; what they experience is simply music that seems to know what's happening, swelling during combat, softening during exploration, shifting the moment a story beat lands, without any obvious seams between one musical state and the next.

    That seamlessness is the payoff of years of work that began long before the game had a finished shape: early thematic sketches from concept art, modular systems built to handle unpredictable pacing, adaptive middleware translating gameplay data into musical cues, and repeated rounds of revision as the game evolved around the music rather than the other way around.

    Recognizing this process changes how a soundtrack can be appreciated, not just as a collection of pleasant melodies but as a genuinely engineered system, one designed years in advance to remain flexible enough to serve a game that, at the time the first notes were written, didn't yet fully exist.

    How Temp Tracks Shape (and Mislead) Early Direction

    Before a composer is brought on, directors often build a rough cut using a 'temp track' of existing music from other games or films to convey the intended mood. This placeholder music sets emotional expectations for the whole team, including the composer, who is frequently asked to write something that captures the same feeling without simply copying it.

    Temp tracks are useful for communication but create a well-known trap: once a team has heard a scene set to a familiar piece of music, they can become emotionally anchored to it, making any original composition feel wrong by comparison even when it is objectively strong. Composers call this 'temp love,' and experienced directors work hard to avoid over-relying on temp tracks for exactly this reason.

    Because the composer is working against this anchoring effect from day one, the early composition process often involves as much psychological negotiation with the creative team as musical craft, convincing them that a new piece serves the scene even though it does not sound like the placeholder they have grown attached to.

    Why Recording Sessions Happen So Early

    Live orchestral or ensemble recording sessions, when a game's budget allows for them, are typically booked and completed well before the game is finished, often locking in final performances of themes that may still be re-timed or rearranged later using the recorded stems. This is largely a logistics issue: booking a full orchestra and a scoring stage requires long lead times and firm calendar commitments that cannot flex around a game's shifting development schedule.

    This means composers must finalize thematic material — the actual melodies and harmonic language that will represent characters or locations — much earlier than the final gameplay footage exists to score against, working instead from concept documents, mood boards, and conversations with narrative designers about what a theme needs to convey emotionally.

    The tradeoff is real: locking themes in early risks needing costly re-recording if the game's direction shifts substantially, but waiting until the game is finished to record would often make hiring a live ensemble logistically and financially impossible within typical production timelines.

    The Difference Between Linear and Interactive Scoring

    Film composers write music to a fixed timeline, which never changes once locked. Game composers face a fundamentally different problem: a player might spend thirty seconds or thirty minutes in the same area, trigger combat unpredictably, or backtrack through a space repeatedly, and the music has to hold up across all of those scenarios without becoming repetitive or jarring.

    This is why so much game music is composed in short, loop-friendly modules with carefully designed transition points, rather than as a single continuous piece. The composer has to think less like a traditional film scorer and more like a systems designer, anticipating every path the interactive experience might take and writing music flexible enough to accompany any of them convincingly.

    Some of the most technically demanding work in game audio involves writing multiple layers of the same piece that can be dynamically mixed in real time based on gameplay state — a quiet exploration layer that gains percussion and intensity as danger increases, for instance — all authored months before the actual gameplay systems triggering those transitions are finalized.

    How Composers Collaborate With Sound Design

    Music does not exist in isolation in a game's audio mix; it has to share sonic space with sound effects, ambient environmental audio, and voice lines, all of which are often being developed in parallel by separate teams working from the same unfinished build. Early coordination between the composer and the sound design team is essential to avoid a finished game where music and effects compete for the same frequency range or fight for the player's attention.

    This collaboration often starts with rough frequency-range agreements — reserving certain sonic space for dialogue clarity, for example — long before either team has final assets to test together, based on shared documentation and periodic check-ins rather than finished material.

    When this coordination breaks down, the result is a common complaint in game audio: a mix where combat music, gunfire, and dialogue all clutter the same frequencies and become an indistinct wall of sound, which is precisely the outcome that early, deliberate planning between departments is meant to prevent.

    Why Some Composers Prefer Not Knowing the Full Story Early

    Counterintuitively, some experienced game composers report that having only a partial picture of the story or full game arc early on can be creatively useful rather than limiting, since it forces them to write music that captures a mood or theme in isolation rather than one over-anchored to plot specifics that might still change.

    This partial-information approach produces music that tends to hold up better across late-stage narrative revisions, since a theme built around a character's core emotional identity rather than a specific plot beat remains usable even if the surrounding story details shift during development, which they frequently do.

    Directors and composers who work this way deliberately structure their early creative briefs around emotional and thematic touchstones rather than plot summaries, treating the score as an emotional throughline that the eventual story will be built around rather than one that strictly follows it.

    Why Franchise Scores Face a Different Constraint

    Composers writing for an established franchise face a distinct pressure absent from original projects: existing fan expectations around beloved themes, meaning early compositional decisions have to balance honoring recognizable musical motifs against the temptation to simply repeat what worked before rather than developing the material further.

    This tension is often resolved through variation rather than pure repetition or pure novelty, reworking an established theme's harmonic structure or instrumentation for a new entry while keeping the melodic core intact enough that longtime fans recognize it within the first few notes.

    Game soundtracks get written before the game is finished because there's no other way to build music that can survive the fundamental unpredictability of interactive entertainment — a player's pacing, choices, and skill level shape their experience in ways no fixed film timeline ever has to account for. Composers work from concept art, gray-box levels, and design documents rather than finished footage, build modular systems instead of linear cues, and lean on adaptive middleware to let that music respond to gameplay in real time, all while accepting that much of what they write early on may need to change as the game itself keeps evolving around it. The result, when it works, is a soundtrack that feels alive and responsive rather than simply looped, a genuine engineering achievement built years ahead of the game it was written to serve.

    FAQ

    Why can't game composers just write music like film composers do?

    Games don't have a fixed timeline the way films do — how long a player spends in any scene depends on their own choices and pace, so music has to be built as flexible, modular systems rather than one fixed sequence timed to a locked picture.

    What is adaptive audio middleware?

    It's specialized software, such as Wwise or FMOD, that lets composers define rules for how musical layers trigger and blend based on real-time gameplay events, without needing a programmer to hand-code every musical change.

    Do composers really write music for levels that aren't finished yet?

    Yes — much composing happens against unfinished "gray-box" levels made of placeholder geometry, using written briefs and concept art to imagine the finished environment the music will eventually support.

    Does game music get revised a lot during development?

    Often extensively. As levels, mechanics, and pacing change during iterative game development, music composed early may need to be reworked, shortened, extended, or repurposed to fit the game's evolving final form.

    Why do game soundtracks often use short, repeatable themes instead of long compositions?

    Short, flexible motifs can be reorchestrated, retempoed, and recombined more easily as a game changes during development, and they hold up better across many different, unpredictable playthroughs than one long, fixed piece would.
