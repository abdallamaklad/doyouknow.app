What Is a US LLC, and Why Non-US Founders Form One

    • An LLC Is a Legal Wrapper, Not a Tax Status

    A limited liability company, or LLC, is a US business structure that legally separates its owner's personal assets from the business's debts and obligations. It is a state-level legal entity, created and governed under state law, not a federal tax category on its own.

    That distinction matters immediately for a non-US founder: forming an LLC decides how the business is legally structured, not automatically whether or how much US tax is owed. Those two questions are related but genuinely separate, and conflating them is the single most common misconception about the entity.

    Where the LLC Concept Fits Among US Business Structures

    US businesses commonly take one of a few legal forms: a sole proprietorship with no separation between owner and business, a corporation with shareholders and formal governance, or an LLC, which sits between the two, offering liability protection with far less administrative overhead than a corporation.

    For a solo founder or a small team, the LLC has become the default choice specifically because it avoids corporate formalities like a board of directors or mandatory shareholder meetings while still shielding personal assets from business liability.

    What Limited Liability Actually Protects Against

    The core promise of an LLC is that if the business is sued or cannot pay a debt, the owner's personal bank account, home, and other personal assets are generally shielded, with the business's own assets bearing the loss instead, up to the structure's legal limits.

    This protection is not absolute. Courts can disregard it, a legal concept called piercing the corporate veil, if the owner mixes personal and business finances or ignores basic entity formalities, which is why keeping the two genuinely separate matters even for a one-person LLC.

    Why a Non-US Person Can Legally Own a US LLC

    US law does not require an LLC owner, called a member, to be a US citizen or resident. A person anywhere in the world, including the UAE, Saudi Arabia, or Egypt, can form and fully own a US LLC without ever setting foot in the country.

    This openness is a deliberate feature of the US business-formation system, which is largely designed at the state level to attract capital and entrepreneurs regardless of nationality, rather than a loophole or an oversight in the law.

    Why Delaware Became the Default Choice

    Delaware has built a specialized business court, the Court of Chancery, and a body of corporate case law developed over more than a century, which gives founders and investors a high degree of predictability about how business disputes will be resolved.

    A large share of US corporations, and many LLCs, are formed in Delaware for exactly this reason: a well-understood legal framework, rather than any tax advantage specific to non-residents, is Delaware's actual draw.

    Why Wyoming Attracts a Different Kind of Founder

    Wyoming markets itself on low, flat state fees, strong owner-privacy protections that do not require publicly listing member names in most filings, and no state-level corporate or personal income tax, which appeals to a solo founder who does not need Delaware's investor-facing legal apparatus.

    Wyoming's pitch is essentially administrative simplicity and lower ongoing cost, which fits a freelancer or solopreneur forming an LLC mainly to invoice clients and access US payment infrastructure, rather than a startup planning to raise venture capital.

    Neither State's LLC Automatically Owes State Income Tax

    Delaware and Wyoming both allow an LLC that does not conduct business within the state itself, and is owned by a non-resident, to avoid state-level income tax on income earned outside that state, which is part of why both states host so many out-of-state and foreign-owned LLCs.

    This is a state tax point specifically, separate from federal tax obligations, and it does not mean the LLC or its owner has no US tax exposure at all; it only addresses the state layer of the picture.

    The Real Federal Tax Question Is About the Source of Income

    Whether a non-US owner owes US federal income tax on an LLC's earnings generally depends on whether the income counts as effectively connected with a US trade or business, a specific tax concept, not simply on the fact that a US entity exists on paper.

    A non-US freelancer who performs all the actual work from outside the United States, for clients who are also outside the US, and has no US employees or physical presence, often falls outside that category, though the analysis genuinely depends on individual facts and warrants a qualified opinion.

    The One Filing Most Guides Leave Out: Form 5472

    A US LLC that is wholly owned by a single non-US person is treated, for this specific reporting purpose, as if it were a corporation and generally must file Form 5472 alongside a pro forma Form 1120 every year, reporting transactions between the LLC and its foreign owner.

    This filing is required even in a year when the LLC earned no income and owes no US tax, and it is purely informational. Skipping it because no tax was owed is a mistake that has nothing to do with whether tax was actually due.

    Why Skipping Form 5472 Is an Expensive Mistake

    The penalty for failing to file Form 5472 when required starts at a flat amount per form per year, and can increase further if the failure continues after the IRS sends notice, regardless of whether any actual tax liability existed for that year.

    This is precisely why the earlier point about an LLC not automatically creating a tax obligation needs a caveat: it may not create an income tax bill, but it very often creates a reporting obligation, and the penalty for missing that reporting is unrelated to how small or nonexistent the underlying business activity was.

    The Genuine Reason Founders Cite: US Client Trust

    A US-based client, particularly a company with a formal vendor-onboarding process, often finds it simpler to contract with a US LLC than with an individual overseas, since a US entity slots cleanly into existing procurement, invoicing, and tax-form workflows built around domestic vendors.

    This is less about legal necessity and more about friction reduction: an LLC signals a level of formality that can shorten a procurement approval process or make a freelancer look like a more established vendor in a competitive bid.

    The Genuine Reason Founders Cite: Payment Processor Access

    Several major US payment processors and platforms, including certain Stripe products and some banking-as-a-service tools, are built primarily around US business entities and are easier, sometimes exclusively available, to a founder who holds a US LLC with an Employer Identification Number.

    Without a US entity, a non-US founder is often restricted to a narrower set of international-friendly payment tools, which can matter directly for a business planning to sell software or services to a US-heavy customer base.

    The Genuine Reason Founders Cite: Intellectual Property Protection

    Holding a trademark, contract, or piece of software intellectual property under a formal US LLC rather than an individual's name creates a clearer legal owner of that property, which matters when licensing it, selling the business later, or enforcing rights against infringement.

    US trademark registration in particular is generally handled at the federal level through the US Patent and Trademark Office and is more straightforward to pursue and defend when the applicant is a registered business entity rather than a foreign individual.

    The Misconception: An LLC Is Not a US Visa or Residency Path

    Forming an LLC gives the owner no immigration status, no right to work inside the United States, and no path to a visa on its own. Owning a US business and being legally permitted to live or work there are entirely separate legal questions handled by different bodies of law.

    A non-US founder can own, operate, and profit from a US LLC indefinitely while never having any US immigration status at all, which surprises some first-time founders who conflate business ownership with a right to reside in the country.

    The Misconception: Forming an LLC Does Not Grant a US Tax ID Automatically

    An LLC needs an Employer Identification Number, issued by the IRS, to open a US bank account, work with most payment processors, and file the required informational returns. This number does not arrive automatically the moment the LLC is registered with the state.

    A non-US founder without a US Social Security Number faces a slower EIN application path than a US resident, typically requiring a phone application or a mailed form rather than the instant online process available to US persons, which is worth planning for ahead of any deadline.

    The Misconception: A US LLC Does Not Automatically Shield Home-Country Tax

    Forming a US LLC has no bearing on tax obligations owed in the founder's own country of residence. UAE, Saudi, and Egyptian tax rules apply based on residency and local law, entirely independent of whatever US entity structure the same person also happens to own.

    A founder who assumes a US LLC removes or reduces home-country tax exposure is making a decision based on a misunderstanding; the two tax systems apply their own rules independently and neither automatically defers to the other.

    Single-Member Versus Multi-Member LLCs

    A single-member LLC has one owner and is, by default, treated by the IRS as a disregarded entity for tax purposes, meaning its activity is reported on the owner's own return rather than a separate corporate one. A multi-member LLC is by default taxed as a partnership instead.

    Both structures can elect to be taxed differently, as a corporation for example, but the default treatment differs meaningfully between the two, which is a relevant distinction for a solo founder deciding whether to bring in a co-founder as a formal LLC member.

    What Forming an LLC Actually Involves

    Forming an LLC generally requires choosing a state, filing formation documents called Articles of Organization with that state, appointing a registered agent inside the state, and paying a state filing fee, all of which can be completed remotely without visiting the US.

    Many founders use a formation service to handle these steps, which bundles the registered agent, filing, and often the EIN application into a single package, though every step can also be done independently by anyone willing to work directly with the state's filing office.

    The Ongoing Obligations After Formation

    An LLC does not end at formation. Most states require an annual report and a recurring franchise or annual fee to keep the entity in good standing, and the registered agent requirement is continuous, not a one-time setup step.

    On top of state obligations, the federal informational filing described earlier, Form 5472 with a pro forma Form 1120, is an annual requirement for a foreign-owned single-member LLC, and a beneficial-ownership report may also apply depending on current federal rules at filing time.

    Opening a US Bank Account as a Non-Resident Owner

    Some US banks require the account holder to appear in person to open a business account, which historically was a real obstacle for a non-resident founder. A growing number of banks and fintech platforms now offer remote account opening specifically built for non-resident LLC owners.

    The requirements typically include the LLC's formation documents, its EIN confirmation letter, and identity verification for the owner, and the process timeline varies significantly by provider, from same-day approval to several weeks of review.

    An LLC Versus Staying an Individual Freelancer

    A freelancer can legally invoice US clients directly as an individual without any US entity at all, and many do so successfully for years. The LLC becomes a genuine advantage specifically when a client's procurement process demands a business vendor, or when payment-processor access is a real blocker.

    Forming an entity purely for the appearance of formality, without a concrete client, banking, or IP reason, adds ongoing cost and compliance obligations without a corresponding benefit, which is worth weighing honestly before filing.

    How an LLC Interacts With Freelance Platforms

    Platforms like Upwork and Fiverr generally allow a freelancer to operate and receive payments as an individual without requiring a formal US entity, since the platform itself, not a formal procurement process, handles the client relationship and payment routing.

    An LLC becomes more relevant for a freelancer moving away from platforms toward direct client relationships, where a client's own onboarding requirements, rather than a marketplace's simpler individual-seller model, start to matter.

    The Cost Reality of Forming and Maintaining an LLC

    State filing fees, registered agent service, and annual report fees together represent a real, recurring cost, generally a few hundred dollars a year depending on the state and whether a formation service is used, which should be weighed against the specific benefit the entity provides.

    A founder billing a modest, occasional amount to US clients may find the ongoing cost of maintaining an LLC outweighs the benefit, while a founder building a real US-facing revenue stream typically finds it a worthwhile, comparatively small expense.

    Why Some Founders Choose a Different Entity Type Instead

    A founder planning to raise venture capital from US investors often forms a Delaware C-corporation instead of an LLC, since most US institutional investors are structurally set up to invest in corporations with stock, not LLC membership interests.

    This is a meaningfully different decision from the freelancer or solo-founder case this article focuses on; an LLC generally remains the simpler, cheaper choice unless outside equity investment from US venture funds is specifically the plan.

    What a Good Formation Decision Looks Like in Practice

    A founder who can name the specific client, payment processor, or IP reason driving the decision, and has budgeted for the ongoing state and federal compliance cost, is forming the LLC for a concrete reason rather than out of vague ambition about looking more professional.

    Talking to a cross-border tax professional before filing, specifically about the Form 5472 obligation and any home-country tax interaction, costs far less than discovering either issue after the fact.

    How Home-Country Reporting Sometimes Interacts With a US LLC

    Some countries require residents to disclose foreign business ownership or foreign bank accounts on their own domestic tax or financial filings, entirely separate from anything the US requires. A founder should check this obligation under their own country's rules, not assume US filings cover it.

    This is another place where the earlier point about two independent tax systems applies directly: US compliance and home-country compliance are two separate checklists, and completing one says nothing about the other.

    What an Operating Agreement Actually Does

    An operating agreement is an internal document that spells out ownership percentages, decision-making authority, and what happens if a member leaves or the LLC needs to wind down. Most states do not require filing it publicly, but nearly all recommend having one on record.

    For a single-member LLC it looks like a formality, but it still matters: banks and payment processors sometimes request it during onboarding, and it is one of the documents courts look at when deciding whether an owner genuinely respected the entity's separateness.

    When a Non-US LLC Owner Actually Triggers State Income Tax

    The state-tax exemption described earlier applies only when the LLC does not conduct business inside the formation state itself. Hiring an employee physically located there, leasing office space there, or otherwise operating within the state creates state-level tax exposure regardless of where the owner lives.

    Most freelancers and solopreneurs never come close to this trigger, since their formation state is chosen precisely because they have no physical operations there, but it is worth understanding as a boundary rather than assuming the exemption is unconditional.

    Why Bank Compliance Checks Can Slow Down a New LLC

    Banks and payment processors run their own anti-money-laundering and know-your-customer checks on a newly formed LLC, and a foreign owner with no US credit history or prior US banking relationship often triggers a closer review than a US-resident applicant would.

    This is not a sign of a rejected application; it usually just means extra documentation, sometimes a video call or additional proof of the underlying business activity, before an account or payment processor is approved.

    How an LLC Name Differs From a Trademark

    Registering an LLC name with a state only reserves that exact name within that state's business registry; it does not grant nationwide trademark rights or stop another business in a different state, or even a different industry in the same state, from using a similar name.

    A founder who wants actual brand protection needs a separate federal trademark registration through the US Patent and Trademark Office, which is a distinct process from LLC formation and worth pursuing separately once the business name is settled.

    Dissolving an LLC Is Also a Formal Process

    If a founder stops using the LLC, simply abandoning it does not end the state or federal obligations attached to it; annual fees, reports, and the Form 5472 requirement continue accruing until the LLC is formally dissolved through the state where it was formed.

    A formal dissolution filing, along with a final Form 5472 filing for the year of closure, is the way to actually stop the ongoing compliance burden, and skipping this step is a common way founders accumulate unnecessary penalties on an entity they thought they had simply walked away from.

    What Actually Matters Before Forming a US LLC

    An LLC is a legal tool suited to a specific job: giving a non-US founder a US-facing entity for client trust, payment access, or IP protection. It does not create a tax obligation by itself, but it very often creates a filing obligation that exists regardless of income.

    Forming one with a clear reason, a plan for the ongoing state and federal compliance, and confirmation from a qualified professional on both the Form 5472 requirement and home-country tax treatment is what separates a genuinely useful LLC from an expensive, neglected paper entity.

    FAQ

    Does forming a US LLC automatically mean I owe US tax?

    No. Owing US federal income tax depends on whether the LLC's income counts as effectively connected with a US trade or business, not simply on the entity's existence. Many non-US freelancers owe no US federal income tax on foreign-earned income.

    What is Form 5472 and why does it matter for a foreign-owned LLC?

    It is an IRS informational return that a foreign-owned single-member LLC must file annually, alongside a pro forma Form 1120, reporting transactions with its foreign owner. It is required even with zero income, and missing it carries a substantial flat penalty.

    Can I form a US LLC without visiting the United States?

    Yes. Formation is a state-level filing process that can be completed entirely remotely, including registered agent appointment and, in most cases, the EIN application, though EIN processing without a US Social Security Number typically takes longer.

    Why do so many LLCs form in Delaware or Wyoming?

    Delaware offers a specialized, predictable business court and well-established corporate case law. Wyoming offers low fees, strong owner privacy, and no state income tax. Neither state requires the owner to live there or visit.

    Does a US LLC give me the right to live or work in the United States?

    No. Business ownership and immigration status are entirely separate. Owning and operating a US LLC grants no visa, no residency, and no right to work inside the United States on its own.

    Do I need a US LLC to invoice US clients as a freelancer?

    No. A freelancer can legally invoice US clients as an individual with no US entity at all. An LLC becomes useful mainly when a client's procurement process requires a business vendor or when specific payment-processor access matters.

    What happens if I never file the required annual state report?

    The state can administratively dissolve the LLC and typically charges late fees or penalties before that happens. Losing good standing can also disrupt banking and contracts that depend on the entity being active and compliant.

    Is an EIN the same as a US Social Security Number?

    No. An EIN, or Employer Identification Number, identifies the business entity itself for tax purposes. A Social Security Number identifies an individual. A non-US LLC owner needs an EIN for the business but is not required to have a Social Security Number.

    Does forming a US LLC reduce my home-country tax bill?

    Not automatically. The two tax systems, US and home-country, apply independently based on their own residency and income rules. A founder should confirm the home-country treatment separately rather than assume US structuring changes it.

    How much does it cost to form and maintain a US LLC?

    Costs vary by state and provider but generally run a few hundred dollars a year in total, covering the state filing fee, a registered agent service, and the annual report or franchise fee, before adding any accountant fees for compliance filings.

    What is the difference between a single-member and multi-member LLC?

    A single-member LLC has one owner and is by default treated by the IRS as a disregarded entity, taxed on the owner's own return. A multi-member LLC is by default taxed as a partnership, with its own separate informational return.

    Can I open a US bank account for my LLC without traveling there?

    Increasingly, yes. A growing number of banks and fintech platforms offer remote account opening for non-resident LLC owners, requiring formation documents, the EIN confirmation, and identity verification, though not every bank supports this yet.

    Should I form an LLC or a C-corporation as a non-US founder?

    An LLC generally suits a freelancer or solo founder invoicing clients directly. A Delaware C-corporation typically suits a founder specifically planning to raise venture capital from US investors, since most US funds are structured to invest in corporate stock, not LLC membership.

    What happens to my LLC's obligations if I stop using it?

    State fees, annual reports, and the Form 5472 filing continue to accrue until the LLC is formally dissolved. Simply walking away without a formal dissolution filing does not stop the compliance obligations or the penalties for missing them.

    Does registering my LLC name also give me a trademark?

    No. State LLC name registration only reserves that name within that state's business registry. Nationwide brand protection requires a separate federal trademark filing through the US Patent and Trademark Office.
