How Saudi Arabia's Hajj and Umrah Capacity Expansion Works

    • The Program Targets 30 Million Pilgrims a Year by 2030

    The centerpiece target of Saudi Arabia's Hajj and Umrah capacity expansion is hosting 30 million pilgrims and visitors annually by 2030, a figure repeated across Vision 2030 documents since the strategy's 2016 launch as the defining number for religious tourism.

    This target covers both Hajj and the much larger, less-restricted Umrah pilgrimage, and it drives investment decisions across transport, accommodation, visas and mosque capacity, making it one of the most cited numbers in Vision 2030's tourism agenda.

    This Is Almost Four Times the 2019 Baseline

    Saudi Arabia hosted about 8.5 million Umrah pilgrims in 2019, the last full pre-pandemic year, meaning the 30 million target represents roughly a 250 percent increase, one of the largest capacity-expansion ratios anywhere in Vision 2030.

    Officials have also cited a lower historical baseline closer to 8 million combined Hajj and Umrah visitors when framing the target, underscoring that 30 million was designed as an ambitious multi-year stretch goal rather than incremental growth.

    Hajj Numbers Are Capped for Safety, Unlike Umrah

    Hajj pilgrim numbers are managed through a fixed national quota system tied to safety and crowd-capacity limits around the Grand Mosque and holy sites, capping Hajj at roughly 1.6 to 2 million pilgrims a year rather than letting it grow freely.

    Because of this cap, almost all of the growth toward the 30 million target comes from Umrah, which has no equivalent quota system and can be performed at any point during the year outside the fixed Hajj season.

    The Interim 2025 Target Was 15 Million Umrah Pilgrims

    Vision 2030 documents set an interim milestone of 15 million annual Umrah pilgrims by 2025, a checkpoint used to track whether infrastructure and visa reforms were scaling fast enough to plausibly reach 30 million by the 2030 deadline.

    This staged-target approach mirrors how other Vision 2030 programs set five-year checkpoints, giving planners an earlier signal of whether capacity investments like the Haramain rail expansion and new hotel supply needed to accelerate.

    Actual Umrah Numbers Have Already Exceeded Early Projections

    Saudi Arabia recorded roughly 16.9 million Umrah pilgrims in 2024 and about 18 million in 2025, comfortably clearing the original 15-million interim milestone and putting the program ahead of its own five-year checkpoint.

    This recovery is notable because it followed a near-total shutdown of Umrah during the pandemic, meaning the sector did not just recover to 2019 levels but roughly doubled its pre-pandemic pilgrim numbers within a few years.

    The Program Sits Under a Renamed Pilgrim Experience Program

    The Vision Realization Program covering Hajj and Umrah has operated under the name Pilgrim Experience Program in more recent Vision 2030 materials, reflecting a broader mandate that includes service quality and satisfaction, not only raw pilgrim volume.

    This renaming reflects a shift in framing: capacity expansion is presented as a means to improve the individual pilgrim's experience, rather than volume growth being treated as an end in itself.

    The Ministry of Hajj and Umrah Leads Delivery

    Saudi Arabia's Ministry of Hajj and Umrah is the lead government body coordinating the capacity-expansion program, working with the Ministry of Interior on visas and security, and with transport and municipal authorities on the surrounding infrastructure.

    The ministry also regulates the licensed travel agencies and service providers, both domestic and international, that pilgrims must book through, since almost no Hajj or Umrah travel in Saudi Arabia happens outside an officially licensed channel.

    Nusuk Became the Single Digital Gateway for Pilgrims

    The Ministry of Hajj and Umrah launched the Nusuk platform in September 2022 as a unified digital gateway for visas, permits, accommodation and package bookings, replacing a more fragmented system of separate agency and government portals.

    Nusuk supports multiple languages and integrates directly with other government systems, letting a pilgrim apply for a visa, book a hotel and arrange transport through a single platform rather than multiple disconnected processes.

    E-Visas Replaced a Slower, Paper-Based Process

    Digital e-visa issuance through Nusuk and related platforms significantly shortened the time it takes international pilgrims to get approval, replacing older paper-based visa procedures that had been a recurring bottleneck for Umrah growth.

    Faster visa processing directly supports the capacity target, since even well-built airports, hotels and mosque expansions cannot absorb more pilgrims if the visa system itself limits how quickly people can be approved to travel.

    Nusuk's User Base Has Grown Into the Tens of Millions

    The Nusuk platform had attracted more than 51 million users worldwide by early 2026, according to Saudi officials, a scale that reflects both genuine pilgrim demand and the platform's role as the mandatory point of entry for most Umrah bookings.

    This user growth is treated internally as one signal that the digital infrastructure underpinning the 30 million target is scaling roughly in line with pilgrim numbers themselves.

    The Haramain High-Speed Rail Cuts Travel Time to Under Two Hours

    The Haramain High-Speed Railway connects Jeddah, Makkah, Madinah and Rabigh over roughly 450 kilometers, running at up to 300 km/h and cutting travel time between Makkah and Madinah to about two hours from a much longer road journey.

    For pilgrims performing both Umrah in Makkah and a visit to Madinah, the rail line removes what used to be one of the most time-consuming and congested parts of the pilgrimage logistics chain.

    The Rail Line Opened in 2018, Ahead of the Capacity Push

    The Haramain line was officially inaugurated in September 2018 and opened to the public in October 2018, arriving early enough in Vision 2030's timeline to serve as core infrastructure well before the 2030 pilgrim-capacity deadline.

    Its early completion meant planners could treat rail capacity as a known, fixed asset when designing later phases of the pilgrim-growth strategy, rather than something still under construction alongside the demand growth it needed to support.

    New Trains Were Ordered to Push Capacity Toward 30 Million Riders

    Saudi Arabian Railways signed a deal with Spain's Talgo for 20 additional high-speed trains on the Haramain line, aimed at boosting annual passenger capacity toward 30 million riders to keep pace with rising pilgrim numbers.

    Each existing train carries 417 seats across two classes, and the expanded fleet is meant to prevent the rail line itself from becoming the bottleneck that limits how many pilgrims the wider system can actually move.

    Rua Al Madinah Is the Single Largest Accommodation Project

    Rua Al Madinah is planned to add more than 47,000 hotel rooms on roughly 1.5 million square meters of land east of the Prophet's Mosque by 2030, making it the largest dedicated accommodation expansion tied to the pilgrim-capacity target.

    The project is designed specifically around Umrah and Hajj visitor volume, positioning Madinah's hotel capacity to grow at a pace that matches, rather than lags behind, the rising number of pilgrims the 30 million target assumes.

    It Is Funded and Run Through the Public Investment Fund

    Rua Al Madinah is developed by Rua Al Madinah Holding Company, a subsidiary of the Public Investment Fund, following the same giga-project funding model used for developments like NEOM and Qiddiya rather than the regular state budget.

    This funding structure means the accommodation side of the pilgrim-capacity push is financed through sovereign-wealth investment, while transport and digital-platform components are more often funded through ministry budgets and dedicated infrastructure authorities.

    Green Space Was Built Into the Rua Al Madinah Master Plan

    About 63 percent of the Rua Al Madinah master plan is designated as green area, roughly 83,000 square meters, reflecting a deliberate design choice to balance dense hotel development with open, walkable public space around the holy site.

    This green-space allocation echoes similar livability priorities seen in Saudi Arabia's Quality of Life Program, suggesting shared urban-design principles across otherwise separate Vision 2030 programs.

    Jeddah and Madinah Airports Were Both Expanded

    King Abdulaziz International Airport in Jeddah and Prince Mohammed bin Abdulaziz International Airport in Madinah, the two main entry points for pilgrims arriving from abroad, underwent capacity expansions to handle rising Hajj and Umrah passenger volumes.

    Airport capacity is a direct constraint on the 30 million target, since almost every international pilgrim arrives by air, making terminal and runway capacity as important to the overall plan as hotel rooms or rail seats.

    Makkah's Grand Mosque Has a Long History of Expansion

    The Grand Mosque in Makkah has undergone repeated expansions over decades, including the large King Abdullah expansion project, well before Vision 2030 existed, reflecting a longer-running Saudi effort to grow mosque capacity as pilgrim numbers rose over generations.

    Vision 2030's capacity program builds on this existing expansion trajectory rather than starting from an unexpanded mosque, meaning some of the physical capacity increase pilgrims experience today predates the current 30 million target.

    A Metro System for Makkah Is Also Under Development

    A dedicated Makkah Metro project has been advanced to move large volumes of pilgrims around the city and toward the holy sites more efficiently than the existing bus and road network alone can manage during peak periods.

    A metro system addresses a different bottleneck than the intercity Haramain rail line, focusing on last-mile movement within Makkah itself, where pedestrian and vehicle congestion around the Grand Mosque is most severe during Hajj and peak Umrah periods.

    Crowd Management Technology Became a Safety Priority

    Saudi authorities deployed AI-assisted crowd monitoring, sensor networks and control-room systems around the Grand Mosque and Hajj sites to manage pilgrim flow in real time, treating crowd safety as inseparable from the broader capacity-expansion goal.

    These systems are designed to let authorities identify dangerous crowd density before it becomes an incident, since simply adding more physical space does not fully solve crowd-safety risk during the highest-density periods of Hajj.

    Wearable and Digital IDs Track Pilgrims During Hajj

    Saudi Arabia has issued digital identification, including smart wristbands and linked health and permit data, to Hajj pilgrims in recent years, letting authorities track group movement, medical needs and permit compliance in real time.

    This digital tracking supports both safety and enforcement, since it also helps authorities identify and manage pilgrims performing Hajj without an official permit, a long-standing challenge for crowd control during the fixed Hajj season.

    Heat and Crowd-Related Deaths Have Driven Safety Investment

    Fatalities linked to extreme heat and crowd density during past Hajj seasons, including a deadly 2024 season with heat-related deaths reported among pilgrims, have reinforced why safety infrastructure is treated as inseparable from raw capacity growth.

    In response, authorities have expanded cooling infrastructure, shaded walkways and medical response capacity around the holy sites, treating pilgrim safety investment as a precondition for growing numbers responsibly rather than an afterthought to capacity targets.

    COVID-19 Nearly Halted the Program for Two Years

    The 2020 Hajj season was limited to roughly one thousand domestic pilgrims already inside Saudi Arabia, and Umrah was suspended for extended periods, representing the sharpest disruption the capacity-expansion program faced since its 2016 launch.

    This near-total shutdown meant two full years of the ten-year plan toward 2030 were effectively lost to pandemic restrictions, adding pressure on later years to grow faster than originally scheduled to still reach the 2030 target.

    Recovery After the Pandemic Was Unusually Fast

    Umrah pilgrim numbers not only recovered but roughly doubled 2019's pre-pandemic level within about four years of reopening, a faster and larger rebound than many other pandemic-affected travel and tourism sectors experienced globally.

    Saudi officials have pointed to this rapid recovery as evidence that the underlying capacity investments, from rail to digital visas, were largely in place and simply needed pilgrim demand to return once restrictions lifted.

    Religious Tourism Is Tied to a Broader Tourism GDP Target

    Vision 2030 sets an overall target of tourism contributing 10 percent of Saudi Arabia's GDP by 2030, and religious tourism tied to Hajj and Umrah is considered one of the largest single components of that broader tourism goal.

    This connects the pilgrim-capacity program to Saudi Arabia's wider economic diversification strategy, since growth in pilgrim numbers is tracked partly as tourism-sector GDP contribution, not only as a religious-service metric.

    Holy-City Projects Carry Their Own Economic Contribution Target

    Independent trackers following Vision 2030 progress have cited a figure of more than 37 billion dollars in expected economic contribution from holy-city development projects by 2030, though this projection has not been repeated as consistently as the 30 million pilgrim figure in official government materials.

    Regardless of the exact dollar figure, officials consistently describe Hajj- and Umrah-related development as a deliberate economic sector, funding hotel construction, transport and retail development around the holy cities as investment rather than pure religious infrastructure spending.

    Private Investment Was Deliberately Invited Into Pilgrim Services

    Saudi Arabia opened parts of the Hajj and Umrah services sector, including hospitality, catering and transport around the holy sites, to greater private investment, moving away from a system historically dominated by government-linked service providers.

    The Nusuk Business initiative specifically aims to let accredited private companies plug into the platform's pilgrim-services ecosystem, extending the same private-sector-courting approach seen in Saudi Arabia's entertainment and industrial reforms to religious tourism.

    Accommodation Quality and Categorization Became a Formal Focus

    Beyond simply adding hotel rooms, the program introduced formal accommodation categorization and quality standards near the holy sites, addressing longstanding pilgrim complaints about inconsistent lodging quality relative to price, especially during peak Hajj periods.

    Standardized categorization also helps pilgrims book appropriate accommodation through Nusuk with clearer expectations, reducing disputes between pilgrims and service providers that had been common under less regulated older booking arrangements.

    Visa Reform Extended Beyond Hajj and Umrah to General Tourism

    The broader e-visa infrastructure built to support Umrah growth overlaps with Saudi Arabia's separate general tourist e-visa system, introduced around the same period, since both rely on similar digital application and approval infrastructure.

    This overlap meant investment in one visa system indirectly strengthened the other, helping Saudi Arabia scale international arrivals across both religious and general leisure tourism using shared digital government infrastructure.

    The Program Distinguishes Between Umrah, Hajj and Visit Visas

    Saudi Arabia maintains separate visa categories for Hajj, Umrah and general visit purposes, each with different quota rules, validity periods and permitted activities, reflecting the different regulatory logic behind fixed-quota Hajj versus open-ended Umrah travel.

    This distinction matters for the 30 million target specifically, since Umrah visa volume can expand relatively freely with capacity, while Hajj visa numbers remain fixed regardless of how much surrounding infrastructure is built.

    Other Gulf and Muslim-Majority Countries Watch This Model Closely

    Saudi Arabia's approach to digitizing pilgrimage logistics and courting private investment in religious tourism has drawn attention from other countries managing large religious pilgrimage flows, given the scale and speed of the Saudi capacity buildout.

    While no other country manages a pilgrimage flow of comparable scale to Hajj and Umrah, elements of the Nusuk platform's digital-visa model have been referenced in regional discussions about modernizing religious travel administration.

    The 30 Million Target Remains a Work in Progress

    With Umrah numbers already exceeding 18 million a year and several years still remaining before the 2030 deadline, Saudi Arabia is tracking closer to its overall pilgrim-capacity target than many other individual Vision 2030 program goals.

    Whether the target is ultimately reached exactly at 30 million or a somewhat different figure, officials have consistently used it as the organizing number for transport, hotel and digital infrastructure investment throughout the program's execution.

    FAQ

    What is Saudi Arabia's pilgrim-capacity target for 2030?

    15 million annual Umrah pilgrims, a target Saudi Arabia had already exceeded by 2024.

    The 2020 Hajj was limited to about a thousand domestic pilgrims, and Umrah was suspended for extended periods, before numbers rebounded rapidly after reopening.
