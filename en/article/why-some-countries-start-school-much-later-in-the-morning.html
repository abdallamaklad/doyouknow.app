Why Some Countries Start School Much Later

    • In some countries, teenagers arrive at school as late as nine or ten in the morning, while in others the first bell rings well before eight. This is not simply a matter of national habit or climate. Behind the wide global variation in school start times sits a genuine scientific debate about adolescent sleep biology, a growing body of research linking early start times to measurable harm, and a set of very real logistical constraints, from parent work schedules to bus routes, that make changing a start time far harder in practice than the underlying science alone would suggest.

    Why Teenagers Are Biologically Wired to Sleep Later

    During puberty, the body's internal circadian clock shifts later, a change driven by a delayed release of the sleep hormone melatonin in the evening compared to childhood or adulthood, meaning a typical teenager does not begin to feel genuinely sleepy until noticeably later at night than a younger child or an older adult would.

    This shift is not a behavioral choice or a matter of poor discipline; it is a well-documented biological change tied to the hormonal transitions of adolescence, which means asking a teenager to fall asleep at the same clock time as a younger sibling is, for many teens, asking them to fight against their own physiology.

    Because the delayed melatonin release also affects when teenagers naturally wake up feeling rested, an early school start time frequently forces a mismatch between when a teenage body is biologically ready to wake and when the school day actually begins.

    What Chronic Sleep Deprivation Does to Teenagers

    Research on adolescent sleep consistently finds that teenagers who are chronically sleep-deprived, a common outcome of early school start times combined with a biologically later natural bedtime, show measurable declines in attention, memory consolidation, and emotional regulation compared to well-rested peers.

    Chronic sleep loss in this age group has also been associated in multiple studies with higher rates of reported symptoms of anxiety and depression, increased risk-taking behavior, and a higher incidence of drowsy-driving related accidents among teenagers old enough to drive to school themselves.

    Because these effects accumulate gradually rather than appearing as a single dramatic event, the harm of chronic sleep deprivation in this population is often underestimated by families, schools, and even the teenagers themselves, who may simply come to regard being tired as a normal part of adolescence rather than a preventable condition.

    The Countries and Regions That Have Shifted Start Times

    A number of school districts and, in a few cases, entire national or regional education systems have moved toward later secondary school start times in recent years, generally citing the accumulated sleep research as the primary justification for the change.

    Where these shifts have been studied afterward, researchers have generally found improvements in measures such as average nightly sleep duration, reported daytime alertness, and in some studies, modest improvements in attendance and academic performance, though the size of these effects varies across different studies and school populations.

    These early adopters are often cited by sleep researchers and public health advocates as evidence that later start times are logistically achievable at scale, even though the shift usually requires significant coordination across transportation, staffing, and after-school activity schedules to implement successfully.

    The Transportation Logistics That Resist Change

    One of the most persistent practical obstacles to shifting school start times later is student transportation, since many school districts operate a single fleet of buses on staggered schedules across elementary, middle, and high schools, meaning a change to one school's start time often requires re-engineering the entire district's bus routing system.

    Because later high school start times often require earlier elementary school start times to keep the same bus fleet running efficiently across all schools in a staggered system, the change can create a difficult tradeoff between the sleep needs of teenagers, who benefit most from a later start, and younger children, who are less biologically dependent on it but whose families may struggle with a very early elementary start time.

    This transportation constraint is frequently cited as the single largest practical reason school systems delay adopting later start times even when administrators are otherwise persuaded by the underlying sleep research, since the cost and complexity of redesigning bus logistics for an entire district can be substantial.

    How Parent Work Schedules Complicate the Picture

    A later school start time can create a direct scheduling conflict for parents whose work hours begin early in the morning, since a delayed drop-off time may leave a gap between when a parent needs to leave for work and when a school is actually open to receive students.

    This issue disproportionately affects families without flexible work arrangements or access to before-school childcare programs, meaning that a policy change intended to improve student wellbeing can inadvertently create new burdens for working families, particularly single-parent households or households where both parents work early shifts.

    Some school districts that have shifted to later start times have specifically paired the change with new or expanded before-school care programs to address this exact concern, recognizing that the sleep benefits for students need to be balanced against the practical needs of the families supporting them.

    The Effect on After-School Activities and Sports

    A later school start time necessarily pushes the end of the school day later as well, which can compress the time available for after-school sports practices, extracurricular clubs, and part-time jobs that many older students rely on, particularly in regions where early sunset during part of the year limits how late outdoor sports practices can safely run.

    Athletic conferences and leagues in some regions have had to adjust their own scheduling norms in response to a shift toward later school days, sometimes moving competitive games later into the evening or restructuring practice schedules to accommodate the shorter after-school window.

    This tension between sleep science and the practical time demands of a full school day, including academics, sports, and other activities, is one of the more genuinely difficult tradeoffs school administrators face when considering a change to start times, since there is no schedule that perfectly satisfies every competing need.

    Why Climate and Daylight Hours Also Matter

    In regions with pronounced seasonal daylight variation, the practicality of an early or late school start time can shift meaningfully across the school year, since a start time that feels reasonable during long summer daylight hours may mean students are waiting for buses in complete darkness during the shortest days of winter.

    Some education systems in high-latitude regions have historically built seasonal adjustments into their school schedules specifically to manage this daylight variation, an approach that adds administrative complexity but can help balance safety concerns about children waiting in the dark against the biological benefits of a later start time.

    Regions closer to the equator generally do not face this particular complication to the same degree, since day length varies far less throughout the year, which is part of why the specific approach to school start times differs so much between different climates and latitudes even when the underlying sleep science is identical.

    How Cultural Attitudes Toward Sleep Shape the Debate

    Beyond the practical logistics, cultural attitudes toward sleep, productivity, and what counts as a reasonable start to the day vary significantly between countries and can shape how readily a population accepts sleep-science-based arguments for changing school schedules.

    In some cultures, an early start to the school and work day is deeply embedded as a marker of discipline and diligence, which can make proposals to delay school start times face resistance rooted in cultural values around effort and structure rather than disagreement with the underlying scientific evidence itself.

    Public health researchers advocating for later start times have found that framing the change around measurable outcomes, such as reduced car accident rates among teen drivers or improved academic performance, tends to be more persuasive in these cultural contexts than framing centered purely on adolescent comfort or wellbeing.

    The Financial Cost of Redesigning School Schedules

    Shifting school start times, particularly when it requires redesigning bus routes, adjusting staff contracts, or expanding before- and after-school care programs, carries real financial costs that school districts must weigh against often-constrained education budgets, especially in public school systems already facing funding pressure on multiple fronts.

    Some districts have found that a full transportation redesign can be implemented at relatively modest additional cost by optimizing routes more efficiently, while others have found the change requires purchasing additional buses or hiring additional drivers, a cost that can be difficult to justify politically even when the underlying health rationale is strong.

    This financial dimension is one of the less-discussed but genuinely significant reasons that school start time reform tends to move slowly even in places where both the research and public opinion broadly support making the change.

    What Sleep Researchers Recommend as an Ideal Range

    Based on the accumulated evidence on adolescent circadian biology, sleep medicine professional organizations in several countries have published specific recommended start-time ranges for middle and high schools, generally suggesting that secondary schools should not begin before a certain threshold in the mid-morning to align more closely with typical teenage sleep-wake cycles.

    These recommendations are typically framed as guidance rather than binding requirements, since sleep researchers generally acknowledge that the logistical and financial constraints discussed throughout this piece mean a single universally ideal start time is not equally achievable in every school system or region.

    Even where formal recommendations exist, actual adoption varies enormously, and many school systems around the world continue to operate on start times considerably earlier than what sleep researchers would consider ideal for the adolescent population they serve.

    How Younger Children's Schedules Differ Biologically

    Unlike teenagers, younger children generally do not experience the same pubertal delay in melatonin release, meaning their natural circadian rhythm tends to align more comfortably with an earlier start time, which is part of why sleep researchers generally focus their start-time advocacy specifically on middle and high schools rather than elementary schools.

    This biological difference is precisely what creates the difficult staggered-schedule tradeoff discussed earlier in the transportation section, since younger children can often handle an earlier start without the same measurable harm that early starts cause for teenagers, making it logistically sensible in many districts to shift elementary schools earlier specifically to free up bus capacity for a later secondary school start.

    Some parents of younger children have nonetheless raised concerns about very early elementary start times introduced as part of these schedule swaps, illustrating that even a change grounded in solid adolescent sleep science can create new tradeoffs elsewhere in a school system that need to be managed thoughtfully.

    The Role of School Choice and Private Schools

    In education systems with significant private school enrollment or extensive school choice programs, individual schools often have far more flexibility to set their own start times than large public school districts bound by shared transportation systems and collective staffing agreements.

    This flexibility means later start times sometimes appear first, and more easily, in private or independent schools, which can create a situation where access to sleep-friendly school scheduling is unevenly distributed based on family income or school choice options, an equity concern that some public health researchers have specifically raised.

    Public school systems attempting to close this gap generally have to solve the transportation, staffing, and childcare logistics problems already discussed at a much larger scale than an individual private school would ever need to, which is part of why change in the public sector tends to lag behind smaller, more flexible institutions.

    How Digital Device Use Interacts With the Sleep Problem

    Beyond biological circadian shifts, evening use of phones, tablets, and other backlit screens has been separately associated with delayed sleep onset in teenagers, both because of the blue light exposure suppressing melatonin release further and because of the alerting, engaging nature of the content itself keeping teenagers mentally stimulated close to bedtime.

    This creates a compounding effect: a population already biologically predisposed to a later natural bedtime is, in many places, also engaging in behaviors that push bedtime even later still, which means the case for a later school start time has, if anything, strengthened as device use among teenagers has increased over the past decade.

    Sleep health guidance increasingly addresses both factors together, recommending reduced evening screen use alongside advocacy for later school start times, on the reasoning that addressing only one side of the equation, either the external school schedule or the internal bedtime habit, is less effective than addressing both simultaneously.

    Why Some Reform Efforts Have Been Reversed

    Not every attempt to shift school start times later has stuck; some school districts that implemented a later start time have reversed the decision after facing sustained pushback from parents over childcare logistics, from athletic programs over compressed practice schedules, or from transportation departments over the added cost of a redesigned bus system.

    These reversals are often cited by skeptics of start-time reform as evidence that the change is not practically sustainable, while sleep researchers and public health advocates typically respond that the reversals reflect insufficient advance planning around the logistical challenges rather than a flaw in the underlying sleep science itself.

    The pattern of successful, sustained reform generally involves extensive community engagement and logistical planning well before implementation, addressing transportation, childcare, and athletics concerns proactively rather than treating the start-time change as a simple, isolated scheduling adjustment.

    How the Debate Connects to Broader Education Policy

    School start time reform increasingly sits within a broader set of conversations about student wellbeing in education policy, alongside topics such as homework load, standardized testing pressure, and mental health support in schools, as researchers and policymakers examine the cumulative effect of school structure on adolescent stress and functioning.

    This broader framing has helped start-time reform gain traction in some education systems as part of a package of wellbeing-focused changes rather than as an isolated scheduling tweak, since presenting it alongside other student-wellbeing measures can build a stronger coalition of support among parents, educators, and policymakers than the sleep issue alone might generate.

    As this broader wellbeing-focused approach to education policy continues to spread in various countries, school start times are likely to remain one of the more visible and debated components of it, precisely because the underlying science is comparatively clear even as the practical implementation remains genuinely difficult.

    How Teachers and School Staff Are Affected

    A shift in school start times affects not only students but also teachers and school staff, whose own commute schedules, childcare arrangements for their own children, and after-school responsibilities such as grading, meetings, or coaching duties are all built around the existing bell schedule, meaning a change requires their buy-in as much as parents' and students'.

    Some teacher unions and staff associations have been actively involved in start-time reform discussions specifically to ensure that any proposed schedule change accounts for staff logistics, not only student sleep needs, since a change that improves outcomes for students but creates unsustainable working conditions for staff is unlikely to remain in place for long.

    Successful reform efforts in several districts have specifically included staff representatives early in the planning process, which research on organizational change suggests generally produces a more durable outcome than a schedule change imposed without that input.

    What the Future of School Scheduling May Look Like

    As sleep research on adolescents continues to accumulate and as flexible transportation technology, such as more efficient route-planning software, becomes more widely available to school districts, some education researchers expect the practical obstacles currently limiting start-time reform to gradually diminish over the coming years.

    A small number of forward-looking districts have also begun experimenting with more individualized scheduling models, offering staggered arrival windows or hybrid schedules that give older students more flexibility, an approach that attempts to sidestep some of the all-or-nothing tradeoffs discussed throughout this piece.

    Whether these more flexible models become widespread will likely depend on how well they can be implemented at scale without introducing new logistical burdens, which is precisely the same practical test that has determined the pace of school start-time reform up to this point.

    Why Comparing Countries Directly Can Be Misleading

    International comparisons of school start times are frequently cited in media coverage of this topic, but such comparisons can be misleading without accounting for underlying differences in school day length, commuting distances, climate, and cultural norms around the structure of a working day, all of which shape what a realistic start time actually looks like in a given context.

    A start time that works well in a densely populated city with short commutes and reliable public transportation may be entirely impractical in a rural region where students travel long distances by bus, which is part of why policy recommendations developed in one country do not always transfer cleanly to another.

    Education researchers generally caution against treating any single country's approach as a universal template, recommending instead that the underlying sleep science be applied within the specific transportation, staffing, and cultural constraints of each individual school system.

    The wide variation in school start times around the world reflects a genuine tension between well-established adolescent sleep biology and the practical realities of running a school system: bus fleets that serve multiple schools on a single schedule, parents whose own work hours don't bend easily, after-school sports and activities that need daylight and time, and school budgets that are often already stretched thin. The scientific case for a later start time for teenagers specifically is comparatively strong and consistent across the research, but translating that science into an actual bell schedule requires solving a genuinely complicated logistical puzzle that varies from one school system to the next. Where the shift has been made thoughtfully, with transportation, childcare, and activity schedules planned around it in advance, the results have generally supported the underlying science; where it has been attempted without that planning, the change has sometimes proven difficult to sustain. In the end, this issue remains a clear example of how science can be decisive while implementation still hinges on a complex logistical reality that differs from place to place.

    FAQ

    Why do teenagers naturally want to stay up later than younger children?

    Puberty delays the evening release of the sleep hormone melatonin, shifting a teenager's biological bedtime later — it's a hormonal change, not a discipline issue.

    What is the main obstacle to shifting school start times later?

    Student transportation logistics — many districts run one bus fleet on staggered schedules across all school levels, so changing one school's start time often forces a district-wide redesign.

    Do later start times actually improve academic outcomes?

    Studies generally show improvements in sleep duration, alertness, and attendance, with modest academic gains in some studies — the effect size varies across different school populations.

    Why do younger children usually keep earlier start times even in reform districts?

    Younger children don't experience the same pubertal melatonin delay as teenagers, so an early start is less biologically harmful for them, making it logistically sensible to shift elementary schools earlier to free up buses for later secondary starts.

    Has screen time made the teen sleep problem worse?

    Yes — evening device use suppresses melatonin further via blue light and keeps teens mentally stimulated near bedtime, compounding the natural circadian delay of puberty.
