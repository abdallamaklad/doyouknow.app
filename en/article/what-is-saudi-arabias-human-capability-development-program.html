What Is Saudi Arabia's Human Capability Development Program?

    • Program Launched as Part of Vision 2030's Broader Push

    The Human Capability Development Program is one of the Vision Realization Programs that translate Saudi Vision 2030 into funded initiatives, focused on equipping citizens with the education, skills and training needed to compete in a diversified, non-oil economy.

    It sits alongside programs covering health, housing and quality of life, but is distinct in treating human capital itself, rather than infrastructure or industry, as the asset Vision 2030 is trying to build.

    It Was Relaunched Under a Dedicated Committee in 2021

    While human capital initiatives existed earlier in Vision 2030's rollout, the program took its current form in 2021 when Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman launched it under a dedicated Human Capability Development Program Committee with clearer authority and targets.

    The relaunch consolidated education, training and workforce-development initiatives that had previously been spread across ministries with less central coordination, mirroring how other Vision 2030 programs were restructured around the same period.

    Prince Mohammed bin Salman Chairs the Program Committee

    The Crown Prince personally chairs the Human Capability Development Program Committee, giving the initiative the same top-level political backing as Vision 2030's economic and giga-project programs, rather than treating education as a lower-priority ministry function.

    This structure signals that human capital development is treated as a strategic economic priority on par with industrial diversification, not simply a social-sector program run independently by the Ministry of Education.

    The Program Covers the Full Education Lifecycle

    The program's scope explicitly spans early childhood education, K-12 schooling, higher education, vocational and technical training, and continuous professional development, treating each stage as a link in one pipeline rather than separate policy areas.

    This lifecycle framing means a change in early-childhood curriculum is meant to connect to later K-12 outcomes, which in turn feed university and vocational readiness, rather than each level being reformed in isolation.

    Early Childhood Education Became a New Policy Priority

    Saudi Arabia expanded formal focus on early childhood education under the program, based on the widely-cited international research finding that skills and learning habits formed before school strongly predict later educational and workforce outcomes.

    This represented a shift in emphasis for a system that had historically concentrated most attention and funding on secondary and university levels rather than pre-school and early primary education.

    K-12 Curriculum Reform Targeted Math, Science and English

    Curriculum reforms under the program pushed for stronger outcomes in mathematics, science and English-language instruction, subjects where Saudi students had historically underperformed on international benchmarking assessments compared to students in higher-ranked education systems.

    The reforms included updated textbooks, revised teaching methods, and expanded English instruction starting at earlier grades, aiming to prepare students for STEM-heavy higher education and technical career paths.

    A Teacher Licensing System Was Introduced

    The Ministry of Education introduced a professional licensing system requiring teachers to pass qualification exams and meet defined standards, replacing a system where classroom appointment did not always require a standardized competency check.

    The licensing push was framed as a prerequisite for improving classroom outcomes, since curriculum reform and new textbooks have limited effect if teacher quality and training are not addressed at the same time.

    Over 115,000 Teaching Licenses Were Issued in 2023

    More than 115,000 professional licenses were issued to teachers in 2023 alone, according to official figures, reflecting the scale of the licensing rollout across the Kingdom's public education workforce within just a few years of the requirement's introduction.

    This figure is tracked as a proxy for how far the teacher-quality reform has reached, since licensing every teacher is a precondition for the classroom-level improvements the broader K-12 targets depend on.

    The 70,000-Scholar Target Defines the Program's Global Ambition

    One of the program's headline targets is sending 70,000 Saudi citizens to study at the world's top 200 ranked universities and training institutions by 2030, a specific and trackable international-education benchmark.

    The target uses global university rankings rather than a Saudi-defined list, tying the program's success to how well Saudi students perform against an internationally recognized standard rather than a domestically set bar.

    It Builds on Decades of the King Abdullah Scholarship Program

    Saudi Arabia has funded large-scale overseas scholarships since the King Abdullah Scholarship Program launched in 2005, sending hundreds of thousands of Saudis abroad; the current program's 70,000-scholar target extends and refines that existing scholarship infrastructure.

    Rather than starting from zero, the Human Capability Development Program narrowed the focus toward top-200-ranked institutions specifically, prioritizing selectivity and outcome tracking over the broader volume-based approach of earlier scholarship phases.

    Vocational Training Got Its Own Institutional Overhaul

    Technical and vocational training was restructured under the program to align more closely with actual private-sector labor demand, moving away from a system historically seen as a lower-status track disconnected from real employer needs.

    This included updated training curricula developed with input from employers in sectors like construction, manufacturing and logistics, aiming to make vocational certificates a credible alternative to a university degree.

    TVTC Expanded Technical and Vocational Colleges Nationwide

    The Technical and Vocational Training Corporation, the main government body running vocational colleges, expanded capacity and added new specializations under the program, positioning technical education as a direct feeder into Vision 2030's industrial and logistics diversification goals.

    TVTC partnerships with international training providers and private companies were expanded to modernize course content and bring vocational graduates closer to the specific skill sets employers in new industries actually require.

    The Human Resources Development Fund Subsidizes Private Employment

    The Human Resources Development Fund, known domestically as Hadaf, provides wage subsidies and training support to encourage private employers to hire and train Saudi nationals, addressing the gap between graduate skills and immediate job readiness.

    This fund works alongside the education-focused parts of the program, recognizing that improving schools and universities alone does not guarantee graduates find suitable private-sector jobs without additional employer-facing incentives.

    Private Sector Job Placement Became a Funded Priority

    The program explicitly links education outcomes to private-sector employment, funding initiatives that place Saudi graduates into private companies rather than the public sector, which historically absorbed a disproportionate share of educated Saudi job seekers.

    This shift matters because Vision 2030's broader economic plan depends on the private sector, not government payrolls, absorbing most new Saudi workers as the state deliberately reduces public-sector hiring growth.

    Lifelong Learning Was Added as a Formal Policy Category

    The program formally includes continuous professional development and lifelong learning as a policy category, recognizing that a one-time education credential is insufficient in fast-changing sectors like technology, finance and logistics.

    This includes short courses, professional certifications and re-skilling programs aimed at adult workers already in the labor force, not only students moving through the traditional school-to-university pipeline.

    Entrepreneurship and Innovation Got Dedicated Support Structures

    The program supports entrepreneurship and innovation culture through funding and training programs aimed at Saudi founders, treating business creation as another form of human capability development alongside formal employment.

    This connects to Monsha'at, the Small and Medium Enterprises General Authority, which runs many of the funding, mentorship and incubation programs Saudi entrepreneurs use, working in coordination with the broader human capability agenda.

    Saudi Nationals' Skills Are Benchmarked Against Global Standards

    A recurring feature of the program is measuring Saudi educational and workforce outcomes against international benchmarks rather than only domestic historical data, reflecting a stated goal of producing citizens who can compete globally, not just locally.

    This benchmarking approach shapes which reforms get prioritized, since areas where Saudi Arabia trails international peers, such as certain STEM assessment scores, tend to receive the most visible policy attention and funding.

    PISA-Style International Assessments Track Progress

    Saudi Arabia participates in international standardized assessments comparable to the OECD's PISA framework to track how Saudi students perform in reading, math and science against students in other countries over time.

    These assessment results feed into the program's broader K-12 reform priorities, giving planners a recurring, externally verified data point on whether curriculum and teacher-quality reforms are producing measurable learning gains.

    The Program Predates Its Current Name and Structure

    Elements of human capital policy existed under Saudi Arabia's National Transformation Program and earlier Vision 2030 documents before the Human Capability Development Program took its present, more centralized form in 2021.

    This evolution reflects a broader pattern across Vision 2030, where early, more loosely organized initiatives were consolidated into named, KPI-tracked programs with clearer governance as the overall strategy matured after its 2016 launch.

    Ministry of Education Is the Program's Largest Stakeholder

    The Ministry of Education implements most of the K-12 and higher-education components of the program, from curriculum reform to teacher licensing, making it the single largest government body executing the program's day-to-day work.

    The ministry coordinates with universities, school operators and the licensing bodies created under the program, though it does not have sole authority, since vocational training and workforce placement sit with other agencies.

    HRSD Oversees the Labor-Market Side of the Program

    The Ministry of Human Resources and Social Development manages the workforce-facing half of the program, including labor-market regulation, Saudization requirements and the funds that subsidize private-sector hiring of Saudi nationals.

    This split between education-focused and labor-market-focused ministries reflects the program's core theory: schooling reform and job-market reform have to move together, or graduates end up qualified for jobs that do not exist in sufficient numbers.

    Higher Education Reform Targets University Rankings and Research

    Saudi universities have pursued research output and international ranking improvements as part of the broader human capability push, with several Saudi institutions climbing global university rankings over the past decade.

    This includes increased research funding, international faculty recruitment and partnerships with foreign universities, aimed at making Saudi higher education itself more globally competitive rather than relying solely on sending students abroad.

    Public-Private Partnerships Reshaped School Ownership Models

    Saudi Arabia has explored public-private partnership models for school operation and management under the broader education reform agenda, aiming to bring private-sector efficiency and specialization into parts of a system historically run entirely by the state.

    These partnerships remain limited in scale relative to the overall public school system, but represent a deliberate experiment in diversifying how education services are delivered, similar to privatization efforts in other Vision 2030 sectors.

    Digital and STEM Skills Are a Repeated Programmatic Theme

    Coding, data literacy and other digital skills appear repeatedly across the program's stated priorities, reflecting an assumption that Saudi Arabia's future non-oil economy will require a workforce fluent in technology-driven roles across most industries.

    This has translated into expanded STEM-focused school tracks, university programs and specialized training institutes, often built in partnership with international technology companies and universities.

    The Program Feeds Directly Into Saudization Targets

    Human capability outcomes are directly tied to Saudization, the policy requiring private companies to hire a rising share of Saudi nationals, since Saudization quotas only work if Saudi graduates have the skills employers actually need.

    Without the education and training reforms under this program, aggressive Saudization quotas risk pushing companies to hire underqualified nationals just to meet numeric targets, which the program is meant to help avoid.

    Women's Educational Attainment Already Outpaces Men's

    Saudi women have had higher university enrollment and graduation rates than men for years, meaning the program's education-side targets are, in some respects, already being met or exceeded on the female side of the population.

    The remaining gap the program addresses is less about educational attainment itself and more about converting that attainment into workforce participation, a separate but related Vision 2030 target tracked through the labor-market side of the program.

    The Program Sits Under the Council of Economic and Development Affairs

    Like other Vision Realization Programs, the Human Capability Development Program reports into the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, which reviews its delivery-plan KPIs and approves cross-ministry budget allocations for education and workforce initiatives.

    This keeps the program's education and labor-market components under a single high-level review process, rather than leaving the Ministry of Education and HRSD to coordinate informally without a shared reporting structure.

    Funding Comes From State Budgets, Not the Public Investment Fund

    Unlike giga-projects such as NEOM or Qiddiya, which the Public Investment Fund finances, the Human Capability Development Program is primarily funded through regular state budget allocations to the Ministry of Education, HRSD and related bodies.

    This distinction matters because it makes the program's funding subject to annual budget cycles and oil-revenue-linked fiscal conditions, rather than to the PIF's separate sovereign-wealth investment strategy.

    Skills Gaps in Emerging Industries Shaped Recent Priorities

    As Vision 2030 giga-projects and industrial programs created demand for specialized skills in construction, tourism, entertainment management and advanced manufacturing, the Human Capability Development Program adjusted training priorities to try to fill those specific emerging gaps.

    This responsive approach means the program's exact training priorities have shifted over time, following where Saudi Arabia's broader economic diversification is actually creating new job categories rather than following a fixed curriculum set in 2016.

    The COVID-19 Pandemic Forced a Rapid Shift to Remote Learning

    Saudi schools and universities moved to remote learning platforms during the pandemic, accelerating a digital-education push that the program had already flagged as a priority, though the shift also exposed gaps in access and instructional quality.

    The experience fed into later program priorities around digital learning infrastructure, treating the pandemic disruption as a stress test that revealed where technology investment in education still lagged.

    Progress Is Published Through Delivery-Plan Documents

    Like other Vision Realization Programs, the Human Capability Development Program's targets and progress are published through official delivery-plan documents on the Vision 2030 website, listing specific initiatives, responsible ministries and measurable indicators.

    These documents are the primary public source for verifying specific figures like the 70,000-scholar target or teacher-licensing numbers, rather than relying on summary statements in speeches or promotional material.

    Long-Term Success Depends on Labor-Market Absorption

    Analysts and Saudi officials alike have noted that the program's ultimate test is not how many Saudis graduate or get licensed, but whether the private sector actually absorbs them into productive, well-paid jobs at the scale Vision 2030 requires.

    This is why the program pairs education targets with labor-market and Saudization metrics rather than treating enrollment or graduation numbers alone as a measure of success.

    FAQ

    What is the Human Capability Development Program?

    A Saudi Vision 2030 program focused on education, training and workforce skills, covering everything from early childhood learning to vocational training and lifelong learning.

    When did it take its current form?

    It was relaunched under a dedicated committee chaired by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman in 2021.

    What is the program's scholarship target?

    Sending 70,000 Saudi citizens to study at the world's top 200 ranked universities and institutions by 2030.

    The Technical and Vocational Training Corporation, the government body running vocational and technical colleges across the Kingdom.

    It aims to give Saudi graduates the skills employers actually need, so Saudization hiring quotas can be met with qualified workers.
