How B12 Deficiency Actually Affects Vegans and Vegetarians

    • What Vitamin B12 Actually Does In The Body

    B12 is a water-soluble vitamin the body uses to make DNA, form red blood cells, and maintain the myelin sheath that insulates nerve fibers. Every cell that divides depends on it, which is why deficiency shows up first in fast-turnover tissues like blood and gut lining.

    Without enough B12, red blood cells grow abnormally large and can't divide properly, and nerve fibers lose their protective coating. These two effects explain almost every symptom associated with deficiency, from fatigue to numbness in the hands and feet.

    Why B12 Is Essentially An Animal-Product Vitamin

    Vitamin B12 is made only by bacteria and archaea, not by plants or animals themselves. Animals accumulate it by eating microbes or other animals, or through gut bacteria, so meat, fish, eggs, and dairy became the reliable dietary sources for humans over evolutionary history.

    Plant foods carry essentially no B12 unless they're grown in contaminated soil or artificially fortified. This is the specific biological reason vegans face a real dietary gap that vegetarians who still eat eggs or dairy partly avoid.

    Fortified Foods Close Part Of The Gap

    Many plant milks, breakfast cereals, and nutritional yeast brands are fortified with synthetic B12, which is chemically identical to the vitamin found in animal foods and absorbed the same way. Checking labels for "B12" or "cyanocobalamin" is the only way to know if a product actually contains it.

    Fortification levels vary widely between brands and countries, so relying on fortified food alone without checking total daily intake can still leave a vegan under the recommended amount, especially if serving sizes are small.

    How The Body Normally Absorbs B12

    Absorbing B12 from food requires stomach acid to release it from protein, then a stomach-made protein called intrinsic factor to carry it through the small intestine, where a specific receptor in the final segment of the ileum takes it up. Any break in this chain lowers absorption.

    This is why B12 deficiency isn't only a vegan issue: people with reduced stomach acid, prior gut surgery, or autoimmune damage to intrinsic factor can become deficient even while eating meat. Diet is one risk factor among several.

    The Liver Stores Years' Worth Of B12

    A well-nourished adult liver holds an estimated 1 to 5 milligrams of B12, far more than the roughly 2.4-microgram daily requirement, and the body recycles much of what it excretes in bile. These reserves can take years to run down after someone stops eating animal products.

    This delay is why some new vegans feel fine for a long time and mistakenly conclude they don't need to supplement. By the time reserves are exhausted and blood levels drop, damage to nerves may already have started.

    Why Vegans Carry The Real, Sustained Risk

    Because plant foods don't reliably supply B12, a vegan diet with no fortified foods or supplements is a genuine long-term deficiency risk, not a myth or an exaggerated concern. Every major vegan health organization, including The Vegan Society, states supplementation is non-negotiable on this diet.

    Vegetarians who still eat eggs, milk, or cheese get a meaningful trickle of B12 from those foods, which lowers but doesn't eliminate their risk. Full vegans have no dietary safety net without fortification or supplements.

    How Common Is Deficiency, By The Numbers

    Studies reviewed in a 2014 European Journal of Clinical Nutrition analysis found B12 deficiency in roughly 92 percent of vegans and 77 percent of lacto-ovo vegetarians tested in some cohorts, compared with about 11 percent of omnivores, though rates vary by region and how long someone has followed the diet.

    A more recent 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis of functional B12 status among adult vegans found deficiency was common but variable across studies, largely depending on whether participants were already supplementing. Supplementation status, not the vegan diet label itself, was the strongest predictor of normal levels.

    Vegan Risk Versus Lacto-Ovo Vegetarian Risk

    Not all plant-based diets carry equal risk. Studies comparing subtypes generally find higher deficiency rates in strict vegans than in vegetarians who include eggs or dairy, since those foods supply modest but real amounts of B12 even without supplementation.

    Pescatarians, who eat fish, have the lowest risk among non-meat-eaters because fish and shellfish are concentrated B12 sources. The general pattern holds: risk rises as more animal-derived foods are removed from the diet.

    The Earliest Warning Signs

    Early B12 deficiency often shows up as ordinary-seeming fatigue, weakness, pale or slightly yellowish skin, and shortness of breath during activity that used to feel easy. These symptoms come from fewer, larger red blood cells carrying oxygen less efficiently through the body.

    Because these signs overlap with iron deficiency, thyroid problems, and simple overwork, they're frequently dismissed or misattributed for months before anyone requests a B12 blood test, which delays treatment.

    Megaloblastic Anemia, The Classic Blood Finding

    When B12 runs low, developing red blood cells can't complete DNA synthesis properly, so they grow abnormally large without dividing at a normal rate. This produces megaloblastic anemia, visible on a blood smear as fewer, oversized, immature-looking red cells.

    Folate deficiency causes the identical blood picture, which is why doctors typically test both folate and B12 levels together before diagnosing the cause. Correcting only folate while B12 stays low can improve the blood picture while nerve damage keeps progressing.

    Numbness, Tingling, And Nerve-Related Symptoms

    B12 is required to build and maintain myelin, the fatty sheath around nerve fibers that speeds and insulates nerve signals. Deficiency degrades myelin in the spinal cord and peripheral nerves, producing tingling or numbness that typically starts in the fingers and toes.

    This nerve involvement can appear before anemia does, particularly in people with adequate folate intake, so normal blood counts don't rule out a developing B12 problem if neurological symptoms are present.

    Nerve Damage Can Become Permanent

    Peripheral neuropathy and spinal cord changes from B12 deficiency, sometimes described as subacute combined degeneration, generally improve with treatment when caught early. The longer the deficiency goes uncorrected, the smaller the chance of full neurological recovery.

    Case reports and clinical reviews describe some patients left with lasting numbness, balance problems, or gait difficulty despite B12 replacement, which is the clinical argument for testing and treating deficiency promptly rather than waiting for symptoms to worsen.

    Mood, Memory, And Cognitive Symptoms

    Because B12 also supports normal brain function, deficiency has been linked to depression, irritability, difficulty concentrating, and memory problems, particularly in older adults. These symptoms can be mistaken for unrelated mental health conditions or normal aging.

    Research in this area shows association more clearly than proven causation in every case, so clinicians generally treat cognitive symptoms as a reason to test B12 levels rather than assuming they will always resolve once deficiency is corrected.

    Folic Acid Fortification Can Mask The Anemia

    In countries that fortify flour with folic acid, dietary folate intake is often high enough to correct the anemia part of B12 deficiency while doing nothing for the nerve damage, because folate and B12 act on separate but overlapping steps of red blood cell production.

    This means a vegan with good folate intake from fortified grains, beans, and greens could have completely normal-looking blood counts while a B12-related neurological problem quietly develops underneath, which is one reason clinicians recommend testing B12 specifically rather than relying on a standard blood count alone.

    Mouth And Tongue Changes

    A sore, smooth, or beefy-red tongue, known as glossitis, along with mouth ulcers, is a recognized but less commonly discussed sign of B12 deficiency. It results from the same rapid-turnover cell effect that shows up in blood cells.

    These oral symptoms often prompt a dentist or doctor visit before anemia or neurological symptoms are recognized, making them a useful, if easy to overlook, early clue in someone following a plant-based diet.

    Vegan Mothers And Breastfed Infants

    Infants rely entirely on their mother's B12 stores and breast milk content, and a vegan mother who isn't adequately supplemented can have breast milk too low in B12 to meet her baby's needs. Reported infant cases show deficiency developing within months of birth.

    A systematic review found B12 deficiency prevalence among infants of vegan or poorly supplemented vegetarian mothers reaching as high as 45 percent in some studied populations, with effects including developmental delay and failure to thrive, making maternal supplementation during pregnancy and breastfeeding especially important.

    Pregnancy Raises The Stakes Further

    Pregnancy increases B12 requirements, and studies cited in reviews found deficiency in 17 to 39 percent of pregnant women following vegetarian or vegan diets in some cohorts, higher than the general pregnant population. The fetus draws B12 from maternal stores throughout gestation.

    Low maternal B12 during pregnancy has been associated with neural tube defect risk and lower birth weight in some studies, which is why prenatal care for vegan or vegetarian pregnant women typically includes confirming adequate B12 intake, not just folate and iron.

    Blood Tests Don't Always Tell The Whole Story

    A standard serum B12 test measures total B12 in the blood, including a fraction bound to proteins that don't deliver it to cells. Some people with neurological symptoms have serum B12 results that look normal or borderline, which can delay diagnosis.

    This limitation is one reason vegans with unexplained tingling, fatigue, or memory changes are sometimes advised to pursue additional testing even when their first B12 number comes back in the "normal" range, rather than ruling deficiency out on that alone.

    Better Markers - MMA And Homocysteine

    Methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine are two compounds that build up in the blood specifically when B12 is unavailable at the cellular level, and both can rise before serum B12 itself drops out of range. Testing them adds sensitivity that a basic B12 test lacks.

    Elevated MMA is considered a more specific marker of true functional B12 deficiency, since it isn't affected by some of the factors that can distort a standard serum B12 reading, though it isn't part of routine screening everywhere.

    How Much B12 Adults Actually Need

    The U.S. National Institutes of Health lists the Recommended Dietary Allowance for B12 at 2.4 micrograms daily for most adults, rising to 2.6 micrograms during pregnancy and 2.8 micrograms during breastfeeding, reflecting the added demand of supporting a fetus or infant.

    This is a genuinely small amount by weight, but because plant foods don't reliably supply it, meeting the RDA on a vegan diet depends almost entirely on fortified foods or a supplement rather than on food choices alone.

    Supplement Options And Typical Dosing

    Vegan health organizations commonly recommend either a daily supplement supplying around 25 to 100 micrograms of B12, or a larger weekly dose in the range of 1,000 to 2,000 micrograms, since occasional large doses compensate for the body's limited absorption efficiency per sitting.

    Cyanocobalamin and methylcobalamin are both widely used forms in supplements; the body converts cyanocobalamin to active forms internally, and current evidence doesn't show either form working meaningfully better for a healthy person without an absorption disorder.

    Why Absorption Efficiency Drops At High Doses

    The intrinsic-factor pathway that normally absorbs B12 can only process a limited amount per meal, roughly 1 to 2 micrograms at a time, regardless of how much is eaten. Beyond that, the body relies on a much less efficient passive absorption route, roughly 1 percent of the dose.

    This is exactly why supplement doses look so much larger than the RDA: a 1,000-microgram tablet is designed so that even at roughly 1 percent passive absorption, enough B12 gets through to cover daily needs.

    The Nori, Spirulina, And Algae Myth

    Some plant foods, including certain seaweeds like nori and spirulina, contain compounds that test positive as "B12" on older assay methods but are actually inactive B12 analogs the human body can't use, and in some cases can even block real B12 from being absorbed.

    Nutrition researchers have specifically flagged spirulina and most algae products as unreliable B12 sources for this reason. Vegans shouldn't count on them to meet requirements even when a supplement label lists a B12 amount, unless it has been verified as biologically active.

    The Fermented Food Myth

    Tempeh, miso, sauerkraut, and other fermented plant foods are sometimes claimed to contain B12 produced during fermentation, but any B12 detected in these products is typically the result of bacterial contamination during processing rather than a consistent, reliable source, and amounts vary unpredictably batch to batch.

    Major dietetics and vegan advocacy organizations do not list fermented foods as dependable B12 sources for this reason. Relying on them instead of fortified foods or a supplement is considered a common and risky mistake.

    Sublingual Tablets, Pills, And Injections

    Standard oral B12 tablets are absorbed effectively for most people, including most vegans, through the same intestinal pathway used for dietary B12, so sublingual (under-the-tongue) formulations generally aren't necessary unless someone has a specific absorption problem.

    People with impaired intrinsic factor production or gut conditions affecting absorption, such as after certain bariatric surgeries, may need B12 injections that bypass the digestive tract entirely. This is a medical decision based on absorption testing, not diet alone.

    Daily Versus Weekly Dosing Strategies

    A daily low-dose supplement in the 25 to 100 microgram range and a less frequent higher-dose option both effectively maintain normal B12 status when taken consistently, according to guidance from vegan nutrition organizations. Consistency matters more than which specific schedule is chosen.

    People who forget doses easily may do better with a routine tied to an existing daily habit, such as taking it with breakfast, since inconsistent supplementation is a more common real-world failure point than the dose itself being too small.

    Why "I Feel Fine" Isn't Reassuring

    Because liver stores can last years and neurological symptoms can develop gradually, feeling healthy is not reliable evidence of adequate B12 status for someone on a vegan diet without confirmed fortified intake or supplementation. Absence of symptoms reflects reserves, not necessarily current adequacy.

    Vegan health organizations recommend supplementing from the day someone adopts the diet rather than waiting for symptoms or a low blood test result, precisely because the gap between running low and feeling it can span years.

    How Often To Get Levels Checked

    Vegan-focused dietetic guidance generally suggests checking B12 status through blood testing periodically, especially in the first year after switching to a vegan diet and again if supplementation has been inconsistent, symptoms appear, or a person is pregnant, breastfeeding, or elderly.

    Testing is also useful simply to confirm a chosen supplement routine is actually working, since factors like inconsistent use or an underlying absorption issue can leave levels low even when someone believes they're supplementing adequately.

    Recovery Timeline Once Treatment Starts

    Blood counts and energy levels typically begin improving within days to a few weeks of starting appropriate B12 treatment, whether through high-dose oral supplements or injections, once absorption or intake is corrected.

    Neurological symptoms recover more slowly and less completely, sometimes taking months, and long-standing nerve damage may not fully resolve, which is the clinical basis for catching and treating deficiency as early as possible rather than after symptoms are advanced.

    The Most Common Vegan Mistake

    The most frequently cited mistake among new vegans is assuming a "balanced" plant-based diet with lots of vegetables, legumes, and whole grains automatically covers all nutrient needs, when B12 specifically cannot come from those foods in any meaningful amount regardless of variety or quality.

    The second most common mistake is starting supplementation but stopping once feeling fine, not realizing that liver reserves, not the supplement's short-term effect, were responsible for feeling well in the interim.

    Vegetarians Aren't Automatically Safe Either

    Because lacto-ovo vegetarians still eat eggs and dairy, it's tempting to assume they're protected, but studies have still found substantial deficiency rates in this group, in part because egg and dairy B12 content is modest and easily undercut by small portions or low intake.

    Vegetarian health guidance generally still recommends monitoring B12 status rather than assuming dairy and eggs alone guarantee adequacy, particularly for people who eat these foods infrequently or in small amounts.

    The Practical Bottom Line

    For anyone following a vegan diet, and for many vegetarians, B12 supplementation or reliably fortified foods aren't an optional wellness add-on; they address a specific, well-documented biological gap in what plant foods can provide, backed by decades of clinical and nutrition research.

    A daily supplement, a fortified food habit checked against actual labeled amounts, and periodic blood testing together form the standard, low-cost approach recommended by vegan and dietetic organizations to prevent the anemia and neurological damage deficiency can otherwise cause.

    FAQ

    Can vegans get enough B12 from plant foods alone?

    No. B12 is made by bacteria, not plants, so unfortified plant foods contain essentially none. Vegans need fortified foods or a supplement to meet requirements.

    How long can it take for a B12 deficiency to develop after going vegan?

    Because the liver stores several years' worth of B12, deficiency can take anywhere from a few years to over a decade to appear, depending on prior stores and diet.

    Is nutritional yeast a reliable B12 source?

    Only if it's fortified, which most commercial brands are; check the label for B12 or cyanocobalamin, since not all nutritional yeast products contain it.

    Do spirulina and seaweed provide usable B12?

    No, most contain inactive B12 analogs that don't meet human needs and shouldn't be relied on as a supplement source.

    What's the recommended daily B12 intake for adults?

    The NIH lists 2.4 micrograms daily for most adults, rising to 2.6 during pregnancy and 2.8 while breastfeeding.

    Can a blood test miss a real B12 deficiency?

    Yes, standard serum B12 tests can appear normal in some people with true functional deficiency; MMA and homocysteine testing add sensitivity when symptoms persist.

    Is B12 deficiency reversible?

    Anemia and fatigue usually improve quickly with treatment, but long-standing nerve damage may only partly recover, so earlier treatment gives a better outcome.

    Are vegetarians who eat eggs and dairy safe from deficiency?

    Not automatically; studies still find meaningful deficiency rates in lacto-ovo vegetarians, so monitoring is still recommended.

    What are the earliest signs of B12 deficiency?

    Fatigue, weakness, pale skin, and mild shortness of breath are common early signs, often mistaken for unrelated tiredness.

    How much B12 should a vegan supplement provide?

    Vegan health organizations commonly suggest either 25-100 micrograms daily or 1,000-2,000 micrograms once or twice weekly.

    Can B12 deficiency affect breastfed babies of vegan mothers?

    Yes, infants depend on maternal B12 stores and breast milk content, and deficiency has been documented in infants of inadequately supplemented vegan mothers.

    Why does the body need such large supplement doses compared to the RDA?

    Because passive absorption without intrinsic factor is inefficient, around 1 percent, so a large dose ensures enough B12 gets through.

    Does folic acid fortification hide a B12 problem?

    It can mask the anemia symptom by correcting red blood cell size, while nerve damage from B12 deficiency continues untreated underneath.

    Do fermented foods like tempeh reliably contain B12?

    No, any B12 found is usually incidental bacterial contamination during processing, not a consistent or dependable source.

    How often should vegans get B12 levels tested?

    Dietetic guidance generally suggests testing in the first year after going vegan, and periodically afterward, especially if supplementation has been inconsistent or symptoms appear.
