What Is Saudi Arabia's Quality of Life Program?

    • The Program Launched in 2018 as Part of Vision 2030

    The Quality of Life Program began in 2018 as one of Vision 2030's Vision Realization Programs, the delivery vehicles Saudi Arabia uses to turn the 2016 strategy into funded, tracked initiatives with named ministries and deadlines.

    It sits under the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, chaired by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, which approves budgets and reviews program-level KPIs across all Vision 2030 realization programs, including this one.

    It Targets Six Interconnected Sectors of Daily Life

    The program groups sport, culture, entertainment, tourism, heritage and urban livability into a single portfolio, on the premise that these sectors reinforce each other economically and socially rather than functioning as separate government silos.

    Coordinating them under one program let planners set shared city-level targets, such as green space and walkability, instead of having sport, culture and tourism ministries pursue overlapping projects with no common measurement.

    Sport Participation Has a Specific 2030 Target

    One of the program's clearest numeric goals is raising the share of the population that exercises at least once a week to 40 percent by 2030, a target repeated across Vision 2030 documents since 2016.

    Weekly sport participation is tracked through national surveys rather than gym membership counts, capturing informal activity like walking and recreational sport alongside registered club and federation participation.

    Baseline Sport Participation Was Very Low in 2015

    Before the program launched, roughly 13 percent of Saudis reported exercising weekly, one of the lowest rates cited when the government justified the need for a dedicated quality-of-life program rather than piecemeal sport funding.

    That baseline reflected a near-total absence of public sports infrastructure for casual users, women in particular, since most existing facilities served elite athletes or private clubs rather than everyday recreational exercise.

    The Ministry of Sport Was Created to Drive This Goal

    Saudi Arabia established a standalone Ministry of Sport in 2016, replacing the General Presidency of Youth Welfare, giving the sport-participation target a dedicated ministry with budget authority instead of a secondary welfare agency.

    The ministry now oversees national federations, licenses professional leagues, and runs public participation campaigns, working alongside the Saudi Sports for All Federation, which specifically targets casual and non-elite exercise.

    Public Sports Facilities Expanded Across Saudi Cities

    Cities added running tracks, cycling paths, and public gyms under the program, part of a deliberate shift toward free or low-cost facilities usable without club membership, which previous sport spending had rarely funded.

    Municipalities also began closing streets periodically for public sport events, a format imported from international car-free-day style programs and used to normalize outdoor exercise in cities built primarily around car travel.

    Entertainment Venues Are a Measurable Target

    The program sets a 2030 target of roughly 613 entertainment venues nationwide, a number that appears in official delivery-plan documents as a concrete measure of how much commercial leisure infrastructure the Kingdom expects to build.

    These venues include cinemas, amusement parks, concert halls and family entertainment centers, categories that were either banned, informally restricted, or simply undeveloped as a commercial sector before the mid-2010s reforms began.

    The General Entertainment Authority Opened a New Industry

    Saudi Arabia created the General Entertainment Authority in 2016 specifically to license and regulate an entertainment sector that had almost no formal commercial existence, since public cinemas and concert venues had been absent for decades.

    The authority approves event licenses, foreign performer visas, and venue permits, functions that previously had no clear regulatory home because the activities themselves were not formally permitted under prior policy.

    Cinemas Returned After a 35-Year Ban

    Saudi Arabia lifted its de facto ban on commercial cinemas in December 2017, with the first public screening in April 2018, ending a restriction that had been in place since the early 1980s.

    The reopening was timed to coincide with the Quality of Life Program's launch, positioning cinema as one of the earliest and most visible proofs that the broader entertainment reform was real rather than rhetorical.

    AMC and Other Chains Entered the Saudi Market

    AMC Theatres opened the first licensed cinema in Riyadh in April 2018, followed by regional operators including VOX Cinemas and Muvi Cinemas, which expanded rapidly into malls across major Saudi cities within a few years.

    By the early 2020s Saudi Arabia had gone from zero legal cinema screens to hundreds, making cinema one of the fastest-scaling segments the entertainment venue target relies on to reach its 2030 number.

    Concerts and Global Festivals Became Routine

    International touring artists began performing in Saudi Arabia for the first time in decades starting around 2017, with the government actively courting large-scale concerts and festivals as visible symbols of the entertainment sector opening up.

    These events also serve tourism goals, since ticketed festivals draw regional visitors from neighboring Gulf states, aligning entertainment-venue investment with the broader Vision 2030 push to grow non-religious tourism revenue.

    MDLBEAST and Riyadh Season Became Flagship Events

    MDLBEAST's Soundstorm festival and the annual Riyadh Season entertainment program, launched in 2019, became the highest-profile recurring events under this reform, drawing large domestic and regional crowds to purpose-built venues around the capital.

    Riyadh Season alone has grown into a multi-month calendar of concerts, sports and family attractions, functioning as a recurring stress test of the entertainment infrastructure the Quality of Life Program is meant to deliver.

    The Cultural Sector Employment Target Is Specific

    Official delivery-plan documents set a 2030 target of 143,746 people employed in Saudi Arabia's cultural sector, a precise figure used to track job creation separately from the broader entertainment and sport employment numbers.

    This target reflects an explicit goal of building cultural work into a genuine career path, covering museums, heritage sites, festivals, and the creative industries the Ministry of Culture was created to formalize.

    The Ministry of Culture Oversees 16 Sector-Specific Commissions

    Saudi Arabia established the Ministry of Culture in 2018 and, under it, 16 specialized commissions covering areas such as film, music, theater, literature, and heritage, each responsible for regulating and funding its own creative subsector.

    This structure let the government professionalize sectors that previously had no dedicated regulator, giving filmmakers, musicians and heritage sites a specific government body to license activity and, in many cases, provide grant funding.

    Heritage Sites Gained New Protection and Investment

    The program funds restoration and protection of historic sites across the Kingdom, treating heritage preservation as part of quality of life rather than a purely academic or tourism-driven concern separate from everyday citizen life.

    This includes urban heritage districts in cities like Jeddah's historic Al-Balad, alongside archaeological sites, reflecting an effort to make heritage visible and accessible to residents, not only to international visitors.

    AlUla Became a Global Heritage and Tourism Project

    AlUla, home to the Nabataean site of Hegra, Saudi Arabia's first UNESCO World Heritage Site, became a flagship heritage and tourism development overseen by the Royal Commission for AlUla, launched in 2017.

    While AlUla operates as its own royal commission rather than a direct Quality of Life Program initiative, it shares the same underlying goal of turning heritage assets into visitor destinations and sources of regional employment.

    Three Saudi Cities Aim for a Global Liveability Ranking

    The program sets a target of having three Saudi cities ranked among the world's top 100 most livable cities by 2030, using established liveability indexes as the benchmark rather than a Saudi-specific measurement.

    This target ties the program's sport, culture and entertainment work directly to urban planning, since liveability indexes weigh factors like green space, safety, healthcare access and infrastructure alongside leisure options.

    Riyadh and Jeddah Have Already Climbed the Rankings

    Saudi cities including Riyadh and Jeddah have shown measurable improvement on global liveability indexes in recent years, cited in Saudi state media as evidence the program's investments are translating into ranking movement, not just spending.

    Officials have highlighted this progress publicly, though independent liveability indexes vary in methodology, and cities still generally rank behind long-established liveable cities in Europe, East Asia and Australia.

    Green Space and Walkability Targets Reshaped City Planning

    Municipal planning under the program added green space, parks and pedestrian infrastructure to cities historically designed around car travel, treating walkability as a measurable input into both quality of life and liveability rankings.

    Riyadh's King Salman Park and Sports Boulevard projects are among the largest examples, designed to add large-scale public green and recreational space to a capital that had very little of either before the mid-2010s.

    The Program Sits Under the Council of Economic and Development Affairs

    Like other Vision Realization Programs, the Quality of Life Program reports to the Council of Economic and Development Affairs, which reviews progress against delivery-plan KPIs and approves budget allocations across ministries involved in the program.

    This governance structure was designed to prevent the kind of ministry-by-ministry fragmentation that limited earlier Saudi development plans, giving one council visibility across sport, culture, tourism and entertainment spending simultaneously.

    Non-Oil GDP Diversification Is a Core Motivation

    Entertainment, sport, culture and tourism spending are explicitly framed as non-oil economic sectors, meaning growth in cinema tickets, concert revenue or hotel occupancy counts toward Vision 2030's broader goal of shrinking oil's share of GDP.

    This framing explains why a program about leisure and daily life sits alongside industrial and financial reforms in Vision 2030's overall structure, rather than being treated as purely social policy.

    Job Creation for Saudi Nationals Is a Central Metric

    Beyond the cultural-sector figure, the broader entertainment, sport and tourism sectors carry their own Saudi employment targets, part of a wider Vision 2030 push to reduce reliance on foreign labor in customer-facing service jobs.

    Programs training Saudis for roles in venue management, event production and hospitality expanded alongside the physical infrastructure, since new cinemas and entertainment venues need staff before they can open.

    Women's Access to Sport and Entertainment Expanded Sharply

    Women gained access to public sports stadiums, licensed gyms, and mixed-audience concerts and cinemas for the first time in the years immediately following the program's launch, changes that had been legally or socially restricted before.

    The 2018 lifting of the driving ban is often cited alongside these changes, since mobility and access to entertainment and sport venues are practically linked for women attending events independently.

    Tourism Visitor Numbers Feed Into the Same Program

    Entertainment and cultural events are counted as tourism draws, meaning festival and concert attendance figures feed into Saudi Arabia's broader tourism targets under the separate but closely linked Tourism Sector Strategy.

    This overlap means a single event like Riyadh Season is reported both as an entertainment-sector success and as evidence toward Vision 2030's overall visitor-number and tourism-revenue goals.

    The COVID-19 Pandemic Interrupted Early Momentum

    The pandemic closed cinemas, canceled festivals and paused most entertainment-venue construction between 2020 and 2021, forcing a temporary reset just two years after the program's most visible reforms had launched.

    Saudi Arabia's relatively fast reopening of large events by late 2021, including major concerts, was presented domestically as proof the entertainment sector could recover quickly rather than as evidence the targets needed revision.

    Giga-Projects Like Qiddiya Fall Under the Same Umbrella

    Qiddiya, a large entertainment and sports city under development outside Riyadh, is one of the giga-projects whose stated purpose overlaps directly with Quality of Life Program goals around sport, entertainment venues and family leisure.

    Unlike the program itself, Qiddiya is funded and delivered through the Public Investment Fund, illustrating how Vision 2030's giga-projects and its Vision Realization Programs operate as separate but goal-aligned delivery tracks.

    Private Sector Investment Was Deliberately Courted

    Saudi Arabia designed entertainment and sport reforms specifically to attract private operators like AMC, VOX and international event promoters, rather than building all venues as government-run facilities, to reduce the fiscal burden on the state.

    Licensing reforms through the General Entertainment Authority and streamlined foreign investment rules were aimed at making Saudi Arabia commercially attractive to entertainment companies that had never previously operated in the market.

    Regulatory Reform Preceded Most Visible Changes

    Before any cinema opened or festival launched, Saudi Arabia had to build new regulatory bodies and licensing rules from scratch, since prior law had no framework for commercial entertainment, mixed-audience events or foreign performer visas.

    This regulatory groundwork, done largely in 2016 and 2017, is less visible than the venues themselves but explains why the program's most publicized changes did not appear until 2018 and after.

    The Program Has a 2021–2025 Delivery Plan

    Vision 2030 programs operate on rolling multi-year delivery plans, and the Quality of Life Program's 2021–2025 plan set updated targets and initiatives building on the first phase of reforms launched between 2016 and 2020.

    These plans are published as detailed documents on the official Vision 2030 website, listing specific initiatives, responsible entities and KPIs rather than only broad aspirational goals.

    Public Opinion on the Changes Has Been Mixed

    Surveys and Saudi media coverage suggest broad public enthusiasm for expanded entertainment and sport access, particularly among younger Saudis, who make up the majority of the population and were the primary audience the reforms targeted.

    Attendance figures at concerts, cinemas and festivals are frequently cited by officials as informal evidence of demand, though independently verified satisfaction surveys on the program specifically are less widely published.

    Critics Point to Social and Cultural Tensions

    Conservative domestic voices and some international human rights commentators have criticized aspects of the entertainment and social reforms, ranging from concerns about cultural change to broader criticism of the government's human rights record.

    Saudi officials have generally responded by framing the reforms as reversing decades of over-restriction rather than adopting an entirely foreign model, positioning the changes within a domestically-led narrative of modernization.

    The Program Is Reviewed and Adjusted Periodically

    Like other Vision Realization Programs, the Quality of Life Program's specific targets and initiatives have been revised over time as some goals were reached early and others required more time or funding than originally planned.

    This is consistent with how Vision 2030 as a whole operates, treating 2016's original targets as a starting baseline rather than a fixed plan immune to updates based on actual delivery progress.

    FAQ

    When was the Quality of Life Program launched?

    It launched in 2018 as one of Saudi Vision 2030's Vision Realization Programs.

    What sectors does it cover?

    Sport, culture, entertainment, tourism, heritage and urban livability.

    What is the sport participation target?

    40 percent of the population exercising at least weekly by 2030, up from about 13 percent in 2015.

    How many entertainment venues does Saudi Arabia want by 2030?

    Official delivery-plan documents cite a target of roughly 613 entertainment venues nationwide.

    Which ministry runs the sport component?

    The Ministry of Sport, established in 2016, alongside the Saudi Sports for All Federation for casual participation.

    When did cinemas reopen in Saudi Arabia?

    The ban was lifted in December 2017, with the first public screening in April 2018, after 35 years without commercial cinemas.

    What is the cultural sector employment target?

    143,746 jobs in the cultural sector by 2030, according to official program documents.

    How many specialized commissions does the Ministry of Culture run?

    16 commissions, each covering a specific creative subsector such as film, music, theater and literature.

    What liveability target does the program set for cities?

    Three Saudi cities ranked among the world's top 100 most livable cities by 2030.

    What is Riyadh Season?

    An annual multi-month entertainment program launched in 2019, featuring concerts, sports and family attractions across the capital.

    Who oversees the program?

    The Council of Economic and Development Affairs, chaired by Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman.

    Is Qiddiya part of this program?

    No, Qiddiya is a separate Public Investment Fund giga-project, though its entertainment and sport goals overlap closely with the program.

    How did COVID-19 affect the program?

    It paused construction and closed venues in 2020-2021, though large events resumed relatively quickly by late 2021.

    Why does an entertainment program matter for the economy?

    Entertainment, sport, culture and tourism spending count toward Vision 2030's goal of growing non-oil GDP.

    Has the sport participation target been reached?

    Progress has been reported toward the 40 percent goal, though official confirmation of full achievement by 2030 is still pending as the program continues.
