    • CST Regulates Telecom and Digital Content Nationally

    The Communications, Space and Technology Commission, known as CST and formerly CITC, is the government body that licenses internet and mobile providers, manages the radio spectrum, and sets rules for digital content and platforms operating in Saudi Arabia.

    CST's mandate covers infrastructure as well as content policy, meaning decisions about internet filtering, app availability and telecom pricing all trace back to the same regulator rather than being split across separate agencies.

    The Anti-Cyber Crime Law Is the Core Legal Framework

    Saudi Arabia's internet conduct rules are anchored in the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, which criminalizes acts like unauthorized access to systems, online defamation, privacy violations, and content deemed to harm public morals or national security.

    The law was designed to secure safe data exchange and protect user rights and privacy online, but its broad language on morality and public order gives authorities considerable discretion in how specific posts or content are treated.

    Online Defamation Carries Real Criminal Penalties

    Posting content that insults, slanders or damages another person's reputation online, including a tweet, repost or offensive comment, can be prosecuted as defamation under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, with penalties that can include imprisonment and substantial fines.

    Reported penalty ranges for online defamation cases include up to one year in prison and fines reaching five hundred thousand riyals, though the exact outcome depends on the specific charge, the harm caused and prosecutorial discretion.

    Content Against Public Morals Faces Harsher Penalties

    Material considered to violate public morals or the sanctity of family life is treated more severely than general defamation under the law, with penalties reported to reach up to five years in prison and fines as high as three million riyals in serious cases.

    This category covers a broad range of content, from explicit material to posts seen as undermining religious or social values, and enforcement decisions here tend to be more conservative than in many Western jurisdictions.

    VPNs Are Not Illegal to Own or Use

    Using a virtual private network is not, by itself, illegal in Saudi Arabia. CST has effectively treated VPN use for legitimate purposes such as business, banking and general privacy as an accepted, ordinary practice rather than a banned activity.

    Businesses, banks and many individual residents rely on VPNs routinely for secure remote work and data protection, and this widespread legitimate use is a key reason authorities have not pursued a blanket ban on the technology itself.

    Using a VPN to Access Blocked Content Is the Real Risk

    The legal exposure comes from what a VPN is used to access, not from running the software itself. Using a VPN specifically to reach content already blocked for violating Saudi law can be treated as a violation of the Anti-Cyber Crime Law.

    Enforcement in practice tends to focus on the underlying blocked content, such as illegal gambling sites or material judged obscene, rather than on ordinary residents who use a VPN for a work call or streaming service.

    Voice-Over-Internet Calling Apps Have a Mixed History

    Apps like WhatsApp, Skype and FaceTime that route voice and video calls over the internet have, at various points, been throttled or partially restricted in Saudi Arabia because they compete with revenue from traditional telecom carriers.

    Restrictions on these services have loosened significantly over recent years, with most mainstream apps working normally for most users most of the time, though occasional throttling or call-quality issues are still reported periodically.

    Certain Categories of Websites Are Blocked Nationally

    CST maintains nationwide filtering that blocks categories including pornography, gambling sites, and content judged to promote extremism, drug use, or activity that conflicts with Saudi law and social norms, similar in structure to filtering used in several other countries.

    Individuals and businesses can request review of a specific blocked site through official CST channels if they believe it was filtered in error or should be reconsidered, though the review process and outcomes are not always transparent or fast.

    Public Prosecution Treats Online and Offline Speech Equally

    Saudi authorities have stated publicly that speech considered a crime offline, such as defamation, incitement or threats, is treated the same way when it happens on social media, rather than social platforms functioning as a lower-accountability space.

    This principle means that content perceived as harmless venting to the poster can still trigger formal legal consequences if it meets the legal definition of defamation, threat or another prohibited category under Saudi law.

    Sharing Others' Private Photos Without Consent Is Prohibited

    Publishing someone's photo, video or personal information online without their permission can constitute a privacy violation under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, separate from and in addition to any defamation charge that might also apply to the same post.

    This applies broadly, including to photos taken in public places if they are shared in a way that embarrasses or exposes the subject, and it is a common source of complaints filed with authorities against social media users.

    Businesses Advertising Online Face Separate Commerce Rules

    Companies and influencers promoting products on social media in Saudi Arabia must also comply with e-commerce and advertising rules enforced by the Ministry of Commerce, including truthful claims and clear sponsorship disclosure, on top of general cyber crime law.

    Influencer marketing has grown rapidly in the Kingdom, and authorities have increasingly focused on requiring paid promotions to be clearly labeled, mirroring disclosure requirements found in many other countries' advertising standards.

    Mainstream Social Platforms Operate Normally

    Twitter/X, Instagram, Snapchat, TikTok and YouTube all operate in Saudi Arabia and are widely used, with Saudi Arabia registering among the highest per-capita social media usage rates globally, particularly for Twitter/X and Snapchat historically.

    Rather than blocking these platforms outright, the government's approach has been to regulate specific content and behavior on them through the legal framework described above, leaving the platforms themselves accessible to the general public.

    Cybersecurity Rules Also Cover Businesses and Data

    Beyond individual conduct, CST and the National Cybersecurity Authority set requirements for how companies operating in Saudi Arabia must secure data, report breaches, and protect customer information, particularly for critical sectors like finance and telecom.

    The Personal Data Protection Law, enforced alongside cybersecurity regulations, gives individuals rights over how their data is collected and used, adding a layer of obligation for any business handling Saudi residents' personal information.

    Political Criticism Faces the Strictest Scrutiny

    Content criticizing the government, the royal family, or state policy in a manner deemed to threaten public order or national unity is treated as one of the most serious categories under Saudi cyber law, with significant legal risk for both citizens and residents.

    This is an area where enforcement can be notably stricter than in many Western democracies, and travelers, expatriates and businesses operating social accounts in Saudi Arabia are generally advised to exercise particular caution on political topics.

    Reporting Harmful Content Goes Through Official Channels

    Residents who encounter harassment, defamation or privacy violations directed at them online can file a report through the Public Prosecution's dedicated channels or the Ministry of Interior's Kollona Amn platform, rather than relying solely on the platform's own reporting tools.

    These official channels allow for formal investigation and potential prosecution, which is a stronger remedy than a platform simply removing a post, particularly in cases involving repeated harassment or serious reputational harm.

    Ramadan and Religious Occasions Get Extra Content Scrutiny

    During Ramadan and other significant religious periods, content moderation and enforcement around material seen as disrespectful to Islamic practice tends to intensify, reflecting heightened social sensitivity rather than a change in the underlying written law.

    Businesses and content creators active on Saudi social media often adjust posting schedules and tone during these periods to align with prevailing norms, which is a practical courtesy as much as a strict legal requirement.

    Streaming and Gaming Content Also Faces Age Rules

    General Commission for Audiovisual Media rules and platform-level age restrictions apply to streaming and gaming content available in Saudi Arabia, and certain mature-rated content can be restricted or unavailable through official regional storefronts.

    Parents and schools are increasingly directed toward government-endorsed digital literacy resources addressing screen time and content appropriateness for minors, reflecting broader concern about children's online safety alongside the adult-focused legal framework.

    Internet Speeds and Access Have Expanded Rapidly

    Saudi Arabia has invested heavily in fiber and 5G infrastructure over the past decade, and internet penetration and speeds now rank among the higher tiers globally, a deliberate part of the digital economy goals within Vision 2030.

    This infrastructure expansion runs in parallel with, and is largely separate from, the content regulation discussion, since fast, widely available internet access and content filtering rules are handled as distinct policy tracks by the same regulator.

    Encrypted Messaging Apps Are Widely Used and Legal

    End-to-end encrypted messaging apps such as WhatsApp and Telegram are legal and extremely widely used across Saudi Arabia for both personal and business communication, despite periodic historical friction over their voice and video calling features specifically.

    Encryption itself is not outlawed for ordinary consumer use, distinguishing Saudi policy from some countries that have pursued outright encryption bans, though authorities retain broad investigative powers when content shared through these apps is implicated in a crime.

    Cybercafes and Public Wi-Fi Follow Registration Rules

    Internet cafes and some public Wi-Fi providers in Saudi Arabia have historically been required to register user identities, a measure aimed at traceability for security purposes rather than at limiting general internet access for the public.

    This kind of identity-linked access is common in the region and reflects a broader regulatory preference for traceable internet use rather than a specific restriction unique to Saudi Arabia's approach to public internet points.

    Employers Increasingly Monitor Workplace Digital Conduct

    Many Saudi employers now include social media and internet use policies in employment contracts, reflecting cases where an employee's public online conduct led to reputational damage for a company or a labor dispute over appropriate off-duty speech.

    Employees, especially in government-adjacent or media-visible roles, should assume that public online activity can factor into employment decisions in Saudi Arabia, similar to increasingly common practice at companies worldwide.

    Regulatory Sandboxes Support Fintech and Digital Startups

    CST and financial regulators have introduced regulatory sandboxes that let approved fintech and digital startups test new services, including some involving data and connectivity, under relaxed rules before a full commercial license is granted.

    This approach signals the government's dual goal of enabling digital innovation and economic diversification while still maintaining oversight, rather than either fully deregulating the sector or blocking new digital business models outright.

    Cross-Border Data Transfer Rules Affect Cloud Services

    The Personal Data Protection Law places conditions on transferring Saudi residents' personal data outside the Kingdom, which affects how international cloud and social media companies structure data storage and processing for their Saudi user base.

    Major global tech companies have responded by establishing local data centers or adjusting data-handling practices specifically for Saudi operations, a trend also seen in several other countries introducing similar data localization expectations.

    Complaints About Telecom Service Also Go Through CST

    Beyond content rules, CST also handles consumer complaints about internet and mobile service quality, billing disputes and contract issues with telecom providers, functioning as the regulator of last resort when a carrier's own customer service fails to resolve a problem.

    This dual role, covering both content policy and service-quality oversight, means residents dealing with a billing dispute and someone dealing with a content-related complaint end up going through the same regulatory body, just different departments.

    Sim Card Registration Ties Numbers to Identity

    All mobile SIM cards in Saudi Arabia, whether for citizens, residents or tourists, must be registered against a valid identity document at the point of purchase, meaning anonymous, unregistered mobile numbers are not available in the market.

    This registration requirement supports law enforcement traceability and is standard practice across most countries in the region, rather than being a uniquely restrictive Saudi-specific policy targeting internet or social media use.

    Content Removal Requests Follow a Formal Process

    Individuals or organizations wanting a specific piece of online content removed, such as defamatory posts or leaked private material, can submit takedown requests to the relevant platform and, where the content violates Saudi law, escalate to CST or the Public Prosecution.

    Major global platforms generally respond to legally grounded government requests, though response times and outcomes vary by platform, and formal legal escalation tends to be more effective than relying solely on a platform's community reporting tools.

    Journalists and Media Outlets Face Additional Licensing Rules

    Beyond general cyber crime rules, individuals and outlets publishing news or journalistic content, including some influential social media accounts, may fall under separate press and publication regulations requiring registration with media authorities.

    This distinction matters for content creators whose accounts function like news outlets, since they can be subject to media-specific obligations that a purely personal or entertainment-focused account would not need to meet.

    The Regulatory Landscape Is Actively Evolving

    Saudi Arabia continues to update cyber, data protection and telecom rules as part of its Vision 2030 digital transformation goals, meaning specific fines, blocked-category lists and enforcement priorities can shift meaningfully over a few years.

    Residents, businesses and travelers relying on internet rules for compliance decisions should check current guidance directly through cst.gov.sa rather than relying solely on articles or forum posts that may reflect an earlier regulatory state.

    International Roaming Travelers Follow the Same Rules

    Tourists and business visitors using international roaming or a local tourist SIM are subject to the same content and conduct rules as residents while inside Saudi Arabia, since the law applies to activity within the country's jurisdiction, not to citizenship status.

    This means a visitor's social media posts made while physically in Saudi Arabia could, in principle, fall under Saudi cyber law, even if the visitor's home country has very different free-speech protections for similar content.

    App Store Availability Can Differ by Region

    Some apps or specific features are unavailable, delayed, or offered in a modified form on Saudi-region app storefronts compared with other countries, reflecting a mix of licensing decisions, local regulation and company-specific market strategy rather than a single uniform rule.

    Travelers who rely on an app unavailable in the Saudi store sometimes switch their storefront region temporarily, though this can create its own account, payment or update complications and is generally a workaround rather than an officially sanctioned path.

    Schools and Universities Set Their Own Device Policies

    Saudi educational institutions frequently supplement national cyber law with their own acceptable-use policies governing student device use, social media conduct related to the institution, and access to certain content on campus networks.

    These campus-level rules operate alongside, not instead of, national law, meaning a student could face institutional discipline for a policy violation even in cases where the same conduct would not separately trigger a criminal cyber crime charge.

    Government Digital Services Rely on the Absher Platform

    Absher, the government's central digital services app, handles everything from visa status to traffic fines and requires strong identity verification, illustrating how deeply internet access is woven into everyday administrative life in Saudi Arabia.

    Because so many essential government interactions now happen through Absher, reliable internet access and account security have become practical necessities for residents, adding a civic dimension to internet policy beyond social media conduct alone.

    FAQ

    Is using a VPN illegal in Saudi Arabia?

    No, VPNs are not illegal to own or use. CST treats VPN use for business, banking and privacy as legitimate. The legal risk comes from using a VPN specifically to access content that is blocked for violating Saudi law.

    What body regulates the internet and telecom in Saudi Arabia?

    The Communications, Space and Technology Commission, known as CST (formerly CITC), licenses providers, manages the spectrum, and sets content and telecom rules.

    Can I be prosecuted for a tweet or social media post in Saudi Arabia?

    Yes. Content considered defamatory, privacy-violating, or against public morals can be prosecuted under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, with penalties that can include fines and imprisonment depending on the specific charge.

    Are WhatsApp and Skype calls allowed in Saudi Arabia?

    Yes, generally. These apps have faced periodic restrictions historically because they compete with telecom carrier revenue, but most mainstream calling apps work normally for most users today.

    What kind of website content gets blocked nationally?

    CST maintains national filtering that blocks categories such as pornography, gambling sites, and content promoting extremism or drug use, similar in approach to filtering used in several other countries.

    Do tourists have to follow Saudi internet laws too?

    Yes. Anyone physically inside Saudi Arabia, including tourists and business visitors, is subject to the same content and conduct rules as residents while in the country.

    What are the penalties for online defamation in Saudi Arabia?

    Reported penalties for online defamation can include up to one year in prison and fines reaching five hundred thousand riyals, though the exact outcome depends on the specific charge and circumstances.

    Is sharing someone's photo online without permission illegal?

    Yes, this can constitute a privacy violation under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law, separate from any defamation charge that might also apply to the same content.

    Are encrypted messaging apps like Telegram legal?

    Yes, end-to-end encrypted apps are legal and widely used in Saudi Arabia, though authorities retain investigative powers when content shared through them is implicated in a crime.

    How do influencers need to disclose paid promotions?

    Influencers promoting products must comply with Ministry of Commerce advertising rules, including clear sponsorship disclosure, in addition to general cyber crime law obligations.

    Is political criticism online treated more strictly?

    Yes. Content criticizing the government or state policy in a way deemed to threaten public order is treated as one of the most serious categories under Saudi cyber law, with significant legal risk.

    Where can I report online harassment or defamation?

    You can file a report through the Public Prosecution's dedicated channels or the Ministry of Interior's Kollona Amn platform, in addition to using a platform's own reporting tools.

    Do businesses have separate cybersecurity obligations?

    Yes. CST and the National Cybersecurity Authority set data security and breach-reporting requirements for companies, and the Personal Data Protection Law adds rules on how personal data can be collected, used and transferred.

    Are all mobile SIM cards registered to an identity in Saudi Arabia?

    Yes. All SIM cards, for citizens, residents and tourists alike, must be registered against a valid identity document at purchase; anonymous unregistered numbers are not available.

    Can I use Absher and other government apps as a tourist?

    Absher is primarily built for citizens and residents managing visas, IDs and traffic matters. Tourists generally do not need it and instead rely on the tourist e-visa portal and standard consumer apps.
