How Esports Team Sponsorship Deals Actually Work

    • A Sponsorship Deal Is Paid Access to an Org's Audience

    An esports sponsorship is a contract where a brand pays a team organization for visibility and association, logo placement, content mentions, event appearances, in exchange for cash, products, or services, rather than buying traditional advertising space directly.

    Unlike a one-off ad buy, sponsorships are usually multi-year relationships built around an org's ongoing content output, so the brand is effectively renting continuous exposure to an engaged, digitally native fanbase rather than a single campaign moment.

    Sponsorship Is the Largest Single Revenue Stream for Most Orgs

    Esports organizations earn money from several sources, prize winnings, merchandise, media rights, and league franchise payouts, but sponsorship consistently makes up the largest share of total revenue for the majority of professional teams.

    Tournament prize money in particular is unreliable and concentrated among a small number of top-performing teams, so most orgs structure their entire business model around securing dependable sponsorship income rather than counting on competitive winnings.

    Jersey Sponsorship Is the Most Visible Slot in the Deal

    The team jersey, or kit, worn during matches and broadcasts is the single most valuable and visible piece of sponsorship real estate an org has, since it appears in every livestream, every highlight clip, and every press photo throughout a season.

    A jersey deal typically bundles the physical logo placement with digital rights, letting the sponsor's branding also appear in social posts, official artwork, and stream overlays, extending the value of that one visible spot far beyond the match itself.

    Primary and Secondary Sponsors Get Different Amounts of Real Estate

    A primary or title sponsor typically claims the largest, most central spot on the jersey, usually the chest, and pays the most for that dominance, while secondary sponsors accept smaller placements on sleeves, collars, or the back panel at a lower price point.

    This tiered structure lets an org sell the same jersey to multiple brands simultaneously, layering several revenue streams onto one piece of clothing rather than relying on a single exclusive sponsor to cover the placement entirely.

    Deal Sizes Vary Enormously Between Regional and Tier-One Teams

    Emerging regional organizations may sign jersey sponsorships worth roughly ten thousand euros a year, while established tier-one orgs with major tournament wins and large fan followings can command deals worth five hundred thousand euros or more annually.

    This gap mirrors traditional sports, where brand value tracks audience size and competitive success, but it is especially pronounced in esports because the industry is younger and its biggest brands have grown their valuations extremely quickly relative to their history.

    Publishers Like Riot Games Tightly Control Jersey Layouts

    In Riot Games' Valorant Champions Tour, the publisher enforces strict, centralized rules on jersey logo dimensions, quantity, and placement, and reserves the central chest area for the team's own logo rather than a sponsor's, treating its leagues as a controlled premium broadcast product.

    This centralized model also bans certain sponsor categories outright, most notably betting and gambling brands, reflecting the publisher's direct interest in protecting the league's broadcast presentation and public image as tightly as a traditional sports league would.

    Counter-Strike's Ecosystem Runs the Opposite Way

    The Counter-Strike scene is decentralized, with no single publisher-run league controlling every team's jersey, giving organizations total autonomy over their own branding. Many teams pack their chest area with multiple sponsor logos to maximize immediate revenue from each spot sold.

    This has produced what fans and industry observers sometimes call a racing-suit aesthetic, jerseys covered in logos similar to a motorsport driver's uniform, a direct visual consequence of a market where orgs, not a central publisher, decide how much sponsorship density is acceptable.

    Skin and In-Game Item Sponsors Face New Restrictions

    Counter-Strike teams have historically taken sponsorship money from companies selling third-party weapon skins and cosmetic items tied to the game, placing those sponsor logos in a spot on the jersey near the collarbone alongside traditional brand sponsors.

    New intellectual property enforcement rules introduced around Counter-Strike 2 have restricted or removed some of these skin sponsor placements from jerseys, showing how a publisher's policy changes can directly and suddenly reshape an org's existing sponsorship revenue.

    Franchised Leagues Bundle Sponsorship Into a Bigger Financial Package

    Leagues that adopted franchising, buying a permanent slot rather than qualifying yearly, bundle centralized sponsorship revenue sharing alongside media rights payments, so a team's income comes partly from league-wide deals negotiated collectively rather than sponsors it signs entirely on its own.

    This model, resembling traditional sports leagues, gives smaller or newer organizations a guaranteed revenue floor even without their own major individual sponsors, though it also ties a team's fortunes to how well the entire league's collective sponsorship sales perform.

    Sponsorships Extend Far Beyond the Jersey Itself

    Beyond kit placement, deals routinely include branded content series, sponsor mentions during livestreams, dedicated social media posts, arena signage during live events, and appearances by players at brand activations, conventions, or product launches.

    Because esports content is produced constantly, practice streams, behind-the-scenes videos, player vlogs, sponsors get a much higher volume of touchpoints with fans than a traditional sports sponsorship tied mainly to game-day broadcasts alone.

    Broadcast Exposure Value Is Measured, Not Guessed At

    Sponsors and orgs typically evaluate a deal's worth using metrics like logo minutes on screen, total viewer hours, and estimated media value, essentially calculating what the equivalent exposure would have cost as a traditional paid advertisement.

    This data-driven approach helps justify sponsorship pricing to brands unfamiliar with esports, translating an unfamiliar audience of stream viewers into a comparable, defensible number a marketing department can measure against its budget for traditional sports or media advertising.

    Non-Endemic Brands Bring a Different Kind of Money Into the Space

    Endemic sponsors sell products used within gaming itself, headsets, energy drinks, gaming chairs, while non-endemic sponsors, automakers, fashion houses, banks, sponsor teams purely for access to the young, engaged audience esports delivers, regardless of any product connection to gaming.

    Non-endemic brands entering the space, including major names in finance and luxury fashion, generally signaled the industry's maturing credibility, since these companies apply the same rigorous return-on-investment scrutiny to esports sponsorships that they apply to any traditional advertising channel.

    Category Exclusivity Clauses Protect a Sponsor's Investment

    Major sponsorship contracts frequently include category exclusivity, guaranteeing that a paying energy drink brand will not have to share jersey space or content mentions with a competing energy drink brand for the duration of the deal.

    This exclusivity is a significant part of what a large sponsor is actually paying for, since it protects against a rival brand appearing alongside the team in the same content, which would dilute the sponsoring brand's association with that specific audience.

    Deals Often Mix Fixed Payments With Performance Bonuses

    Beyond a guaranteed base fee, many sponsorship contracts include performance-based bonuses tied to tournament placements, championship wins, or reaching specific viewership or social media growth targets over the contract's term.

    This structure lets brands lower their guaranteed spend while still rewarding an org that overperforms competitively, aligning the sponsor's payout more closely with the actual exposure and prestige the team generates rather than a flat fee regardless of results.

    Individual Player Sponsorships Sit Alongside Team-Level Deals

    Star players frequently sign their own separate sponsorships, personal streaming deals, individual endorsements, brand ambassadorships, that exist independently of and sometimes alongside the sponsorships their organization signs at the team level.

    This dual layer means an org must often negotiate around a player's personal sponsor obligations, and category exclusivity issues can arise if a star player's individual deal conflicts with a sponsor the team itself has signed.

    The Global Sponsorship Market Has Grown Sharply in a Few Years

    Global esports sponsorship revenue was estimated at roughly eight hundred thirty seven million dollars in 2022, with projections putting it near one point two billion dollars by 2026, reflecting rapid growth even as other parts of the industry went through a rougher period.

    This growth trajectory has attracted increasing attention from major advertising agencies and brand consultancies, who now treat esports sponsorship valuation and strategy as a distinct specialty rather than a small, experimental line item within a broader digital marketing budget.

    The Industry Went Through a Genuine Sponsorship Downturn

    Around 2022 and 2023, several esports organizations reported sponsors pulling back or declining to renew deals as broader economic conditions tightened marketing budgets across many industries, exposing how dependent some orgs had become on a small number of large sponsors.

    That downturn pushed some organizations to fold, downsize rosters, or restructure entirely, and it prompted a broader industry conversation about diversifying revenue away from sponsorship-only models toward merchandise, media production, and other more resilient income sources.

    Venture Capital Investment Shaped How Orgs Chase Sponsors

    Many top esports organizations raised significant venture capital funding in the years before that downturn, based partly on projections of sponsorship revenue growth, which pushed some orgs to spend aggressively on rosters and staff before their actual sponsor income had caught up.

    When some of those growth projections underdelivered, it forced a broader reckoning within the industry about whether esports org valuations had been built too heavily on anticipated future sponsorship deals rather than revenue an organization had already secured.

    Merchandise Sales Are the Sponsorship Model's Complement, Not Its Replacement

    Jersey and apparel sales to fans generate direct revenue and also function as free advertising for whatever brand appears on that jersey, meaning strong merchandise sales can actually increase a sponsorship's value by putting a sponsor's logo on the streets, not just the screen.

    Some organizations negotiate merchandise revenue sharing directly into sponsorship contracts, since a sponsor's own logo appearing on garments purchased by fans blurs the line between straightforward product sales and ongoing brand exposure the sponsor is also paying to secure.

    Regional Markets Have Reached Very Different Levels of Maturity

    Sponsorship markets in Western Europe and North America are generally considered more developed, with more non-endemic brand participation and higher average deal values, while some other regions still rely more heavily on endemic gaming-industry sponsors and smaller local deals.

    This uneven maturity affects how a given regional esports org builds its business, since teams in less-developed sponsorship markets often depend more on league franchise payouts or streaming platform revenue to make up the gap left by a thinner local sponsorship pool.

    Sponsors Fund Costs the Public Rarely Sees

    Sponsorship income covers far more than visible marketing, player and coaching staff salaries, travel and accommodation for international tournaments, gaming houses or training facilities, and the content production team that creates the videos and streams fans actually watch.

    Because these operational costs are constant and often substantial, a sudden loss of one or two major sponsors can force an organization into layoffs or roster cuts far more quickly than it might in a traditional sports business with more diversified income.

    College and Amateur Scenes Attract a Different Kind of Sponsor

    Collegiate and amateur esports programs generally attract smaller, more locally focused sponsors, or sponsorship in kind, free hardware, software licenses, event support, rather than the large cash deals seen at the professional tier, reflecting the smaller audiences these scenes reach.

    This tier still matters commercially, since brands increasingly use amateur and collegiate sponsorships as an early, lower-cost way to build relationships with players who may later become professional talent or influential figures within the broader gaming community.

    Every Sponsorship Deal Must Clear Publisher Approval First

    Because esports teams compete in games owned by a publisher, most major sponsorship agreements require that publisher's sign-off on category restrictions, logo placement rules, and broadcast integration, meaning an org cannot simply sell any sponsorship it can find.

    This approval layer differs by publisher and by game, giving Riot Games, Valve, and other publishers real influence over an org's revenue potential regardless of how commercially attractive a given sponsor might otherwise be to the team itself.

    Gambling and Betting Sponsors Remain the Industry's Most Contested Category

    Betting brands have become a major sponsorship category in esports, drawn by the same young, digitally engaged audience other advertisers want, but their presence has drawn criticism given the youth of many viewers and concerns about normalizing gambling around competitive gaming.

    Different publishers and leagues have responded inconsistently, some banning betting sponsors from official broadcasts entirely while others permit them, leaving orgs to navigate a patchwork of rules that vary depending on which game and league they compete within.

    Naming Rights Extend Sponsorship Beyond a Team's Jersey Entirely

    Some sponsorship deals go further than logo placement, granting naming rights to an org's training facility, gaming house, or even, in some regional cases, incorporating a sponsor's name directly into the team's official branding for a contracted period.

    This deeper level of integration is less common than jersey deals but represents the far end of sponsorship depth, essentially making the sponsor's identity inseparable from the organization's own public identity for the length of that agreement.

    Independent Tournament Organizers Sell a Different Sponsorship Product

    Tournament operators like ESL or BLAST sell their own separate sponsorship inventory tied to a specific event or circuit, arena signage, broadcast overlays, event naming rights, distinct from the sponsorships individual competing teams sell for their own jerseys.

    This creates two parallel sponsorship markets operating at once during a major tournament, the event organizer's own sponsor slate and each competing team's independent jersey and content sponsors, both monetizing the same broadcast audience through different contracts.

    Multi-Year Mega Deals Show How Far the Category Has Come

    Recent years have seen large, multi-year sponsorship agreements between major mainstream brands and top-tier organizations, bundling jersey rights, content series, and event activations into packages sometimes reported in the high eight-figure range across the deal's full term.

    These headline deals, involving names like Red Bull and major energy and beverage brands partnering with leading European and North American organizations, function as public proof points that convince other mainstream advertisers the category is worth serious budget consideration.

    Sponsorship Compliance Requires Ongoing Legal and Content Review

    Because sponsorships touch broadcast rights, publisher rules, and regional advertising law simultaneously, larger organizations maintain dedicated staff or legal counsel to review every piece of sponsored content before it airs, checking for rule violations across all three layers at once.

    This compliance overhead grows with deal complexity, and mistakes carry real cost, since a publisher can fine or sanction a team for an improperly placed logo or an unapproved sponsor category appearing during an official broadcast.

    The Category Is Increasingly Treated Like Mainstream Sports Marketing

    As deal sizes, audience data, and brand sophistication have all grown, esports sponsorship negotiation increasingly mirrors traditional sports marketing practice, structured pitch decks, third-party audience verification, formal contract terms, rather than the informal handshake deals common in the industry's earlier years.

    This professionalization signals a category moving from a novelty marketing experiment toward a durable, established advertising channel, even as it retains distinctive esports features like the influence of publisher rules and the constant, always-on nature of team content.

    Future Deals Will Likely Be Shaped by Tighter Regulation

    Ongoing regulatory attention to gambling advertising, data privacy around younger audiences, and consumer protection standards is expected to keep influencing which sponsor categories esports organizations and leagues are willing or able to accept going forward.

    Organizations that diversify beyond a single dominant sponsor category, and that build compliance processes able to adapt quickly to new publisher or regional rules, are generally better positioned to weather the kind of sponsorship volatility the industry has already experienced once.

    Venue and Arena Deals Add a Physical Layer to Sponsorship

    Dedicated esports arenas built for permanent leagues, such as venues used for League of Legends competitions, sell their own naming rights and in-venue signage sponsorships, a physical-world revenue layer that sits alongside broadcast and jersey sponsorship entirely.

    This physical venue sponsorship mirrors traditional stadium-naming deals in conventional sports, and it signals that esports leagues mature enough to operate permanent venues can access an entirely separate category of sponsor previously unavailable to teams competing only in rented convention spaces.

    Mobile Esports Runs on a Different Sponsorship Map Entirely

    Mobile titles popular across Asia and Latin America draw enormous audiences and correspondingly large local sponsorship markets, often dominated by telecom carriers, mobile payment platforms, and regional consumer brands rather than the hardware and energy drink sponsors common in Western PC and console esports.

    This divergence means a sponsorship strategy built around one region or one type of game rarely transfers directly elsewhere, and it explains why global esports organizations often negotiate separate regional sponsorship packages rather than a single worldwide deal covering every title they compete in.

    FAQ

    What is the single largest revenue source for most esports organizations?

    What is the difference between a primary and secondary jersey sponsor?

    A primary sponsor pays for the largest, most central spot, usually the chest, while secondary sponsors take smaller placements on sleeves or the back panel at lower prices, letting one jersey carry multiple paying sponsors.

    Why does Riot Games control Valorant jersey sponsorships so tightly?

    Riot Games runs a centralized league model and treats its broadcasts as a controlled premium product, enforcing strict rules on logo placement, size, and banned categories like betting sponsors.

    Why do Counter-Strike jerseys often have so many sponsor logos?
